3. Terminology: What is Self-Forgiveness?

Self-forgiveness: A process by which a person reduces negative and increases positive thoughts, emotions, motivations, and behaviors toward oneself after a self-perceived wrong. It is an emotion-focused coping strategy .

Three-component model of self-forgiveness (Holmgren): 1) Acknowledgment of the objective wrongdoing and sincere recognition of it as guilt-inducing; 2) The ability to let go of self-directed resentment and guilt; 3) Self-acceptance — full acceptance of oneself, acknowledgment of one's shortcomings, and demonstration of self-compassion .

Pseudo self-forgiveness: A process where a person uses psychological defenses (rationalization, moral disengagement) to achieve positive self-perception after wrongdoing, without genuine acknowledgment of the wrongfulness of their actions .

Moral Injury: A conflict between one's conscience and overwhelming existential or psychospiritual experience that leads to the loss of a safe connection with oneself, society, God/Higher Power, and the world. Healing requires restoring connections of self-respect, trust, and life-affirming relationships .

Component of Self-Compassion (Neff) Description
Self-kindness Responding to suffering and failures with warmth and understanding, rather than ignoring or harsh self-criticism .
Common humanity Recognizing that suffering is part of the shared human experience, fostering connection and preventing isolation .
Mindfulness Non-judgmental awareness of one's own experience without suppression or over-immersion in emotions .

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: 6-Fold Path to Self-Forgiveness for Moral Injury (PubMed, 2024)

Title: 6-Fold path to self-forgiveness: an interdisciplinary model for the treatment of moral injury with intervention strategies for clinicians
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39654935/
Key Findings:
  • Conscience is the indestructible core of personal identity and sense of agency. When it condemns a person, internal conflict (moral injury) arises.
  • Moral injury is the loss of a safe connection with oneself, society, God/Higher Power, and the world.
  • Healing requires restoring connections of self-respect, trust, and life-affirming relationships.
  • The article presents the 6-fold path to self-forgiveness (6-FPSF) — an interdisciplinary, narrative healing-through-writing process for treating moral injury.
  • The protocol draws on theoretical literature, evidence-based psychological interventions, spiritually-oriented practices, creative arts, and somatic exercises.

📌 Source 2: Online Self-Compassion Interventions - Systematic Review of RCTs (Springer, 2025)

Title: Online Self-Compassion Interventions and Wellbeing Outcomes: A Systematic Review of RCTs
Link: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-025-02606-8
Key Findings:
  • Systematic review of 21 RCTs (2014-2024) of online self-compassion interventions.
  • Most studies showed that online interventions improve self-compassion.
  • Self-compassion consists of three elements: self-kindness vs self-judgment, common humanity vs isolation, mindfulness vs over-identification (Neff, 2003).
  • Self-compassion activates the positive affect system, stimulating calming hormones (oxytocin) and managing physiological reactions to create feelings of safety and connection (Gilbert, 2014).
  • Online interventions offer remote accessibility, timely access, less perception as "therapy," and population-level scalability.

📌 Source 3: ERP Study of Forgiveness Therapy in Alcohol Dependence (Sage Journals, 2023)

Title: Letting Go of Grudges: An Intervention-Based ERP Study Among Patients with ADS
Link: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02537176231203625
Key Findings:
  • Study of patients with alcohol dependence showed significant improvement in total forgiveness score after 6 weeks of forgiveness therapy (from 87.9 to 113.8, p=0.001).
  • Significant reduction in anger (p=0.001) and feelings of shame/guilt (p<0.001).
  • Reduction of negative emotions such as shame and guilt can be explained by the person's ability to cope with negative self-evaluations by forgiving themselves for not meeting their own expectations .
  • The study shows that forgiveness therapy is effective among patients with a history of interpersonal trauma in alleviating their negative emotional state.

📌 Source 4: Ukrainian Research Project on Forgiveness in Military Conflict Zones (International Forgiveness Institute, 2022)

Title: Ukrainian Research Project Verifies Benefits of Forgiveness in Military Conflict Zones
Link: https://internationalforgiveness.com/2022/07/08/ukrainian-research-project-verifies-benefits-of-forgiveness-in-military-conflict-zones/
Key Findings:
  • Study by Kravchuk & Khalansky (2019-2020) involving 302 Ukrainian citizens, half of whom lived in the military conflict zone in eastern Ukraine.
  • Main findings:
    • "The obtained correlations show that the more a person is predisposed to forgiveness, the less anxiety and depression they have."
    • "A person with a high predisposition to forgiveness is characterized by higher levels of decisive forgiveness, hope, emotional forgiveness, tolerance and acceptance of others, mental health, happiness, and life satisfaction."
    • "Hope, happiness, life satisfaction, and predisposition to forgiveness may allow citizens living in eastern Ukraine to quickly recover from psychological trauma, contribute to successfully overcoming the negative consequences of the military conflict and functioning successfully."

📌 Source 5: Psychological Interventions to Promote Self-Forgiveness - Systematic Review (BMC Psychology, 2024)

Title: Psychological interventions to promote self-forgiveness: a systematic review
Link: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-024-01671-3
Key Findings:
  • Systematic review of 21 articles on interventions promoting self-forgiveness.
  • 13 out of 21 articles used interventions specifically designed to promote self-forgiveness.
  • Interventions are divided into self-directed and group-based.
  • Self-directed interventions, especially those based on Enright's process model, demonstrate effectiveness in developing self-forgiveness.
  • Self-forgiveness interventions are also effective in promoting other positive psychological and clinical variables.
  • Self-forgiveness improves psychological health, including life satisfaction, self-esteem, emotional stability, and perceived quality of life.

📌 Source 6: Effects of Self-Compassion Interventions - Meta-Analysis (NIH, 2023)

Title: Effects of Self-Compassion Interventions on Reducing Depressive Symptoms, Anxiety, and Stress: A Meta-Analysis
Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10239723/
Key Findings:
  • Meta-analysis of 56 RCTs showed that interventions targeting self-compassion have small to medium effects on reducing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress.
  • Self-compassion is the ability to be open to one's own difficulties with kindness and non-judgmental understanding.
  • Research shows that promoting self-compassion can reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress by enhancing mindfulness and emotion regulation.

📌 Source 7: Guilt, Shame, and Forgiveness in Abused Adolescents (Acta Theologica, 2014)

Title: Utilising forgiveness to help sexually abused adolescents break free from guilt and shame: A pastoral Gestalt theory
Link: https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1015-87582014000500001
Key Findings:
  • Feelings of guilt and shame can be traumatic, conscious experiences affecting physical, psychological, social, emotional, moral, and religious development.
  • Guilt and shame cause feelings of helplessness, anger, blame, bitterness, and need for retribution.
  • Distinction: Guilt concerns negative evaluation of behavior, while shame concerns negative evaluation of the entire "self." "When we feel guilt, we blame our behavior. When we feel shame, we blame our character and being."

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Critically important for creating a safe, non-judgmental space for someone struggling with self-criticism, guilt, and shame. Compassion is the foundation of self-forgiveness .
  • Patience: 10/10 — Maximum patience required, as self-forgiveness is a process, not a one-time event. The person may need time and return to the same topics .
  • Assertiveness: 3/10 — Very low assertiveness. Cannot push a person to "forgive themselves" — this may cause additional guilt. The role offers but does not insist .
  • Creativity: 8/10 — High creativity needed for creating metaphors, narrative exercises, letters, dialogues that help in the self-forgiveness process .
  • Formality: 2/10 — Very low. Formal tone can feel cold, judgmental. A warm, accepting, human tone is needed .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, compassion, and respect for the person's unique path to self-forgiveness.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From understanding the concept of self-forgiveness and overcoming barriers to integration and maintenance. Based on Enright's process model and the 6-fold path to self-forgiveness .

Session 1: Understanding Self-Forgiveness
Tasks: Psychoeducation: what self-forgiveness is (and is not); distinguish guilt from shame; introduce concept of "pseudo self-forgiveness"; assess readiness; normalize experience.
Expected Outcomes: Understanding that self-forgiveness is a process, not "justification"; reduced confusion between guilt and shame.
Rationale: Education about self-forgiveness is the first step . Distinguishing guilt and shame is critical .

Session 2: Acknowledging the Hurt
Tasks: Safely explore what exactly the person cannot forgive themselves for; acknowledge feelings (guilt, shame, anger, regret) without judgment; narrative exercise (writing about the event).
Expected Outcomes: Acknowledgment of hurt without re-traumatization; identification of key event/action.
Rationale: Acknowledgment of objective wrongdoing and sincere recognition of it is the first component of Holmgren's model .

Session 3: Working with Shame and Guilt
Tasks: Distinguish: "I did something bad" (guilt) vs "I am bad" (shame); self-compassion techniques for shame; cognitive work with excessive guilt; normalize common humanity.
Expected Outcomes: Reduced toxic shame; more realistic assessment of guilt; feeling that "I am not alone in this."
Rationale: Self-compassion (self-kindness, common humanity) is key to overcoming shame .

Session 4: The Process of Self-Forgiveness
Tasks: Introduce self-forgiveness process (based on Enright's model); "letter to myself" exercise (with forgiveness); work with "unfinished business"; take responsibility without self-punishment.
Expected Outcomes: Beginning of active self-forgiveness process; reduced self-punishment.
Rationale: Self-directed interventions based on Enright's process model are effective for developing self-forgiveness .

Session 5: Self-Compassion and Self-Acceptance
Tasks: Self-compassion practices (self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness); "how would I treat a friend" exercise; work with "inner critic"; accept one's imperfection.
Expected Outcomes: Increased self-compassion; reduced self-criticism; feeling of self-acceptance.
Rationale: Self-acceptance and self-compassion are the third component of Holmgren's model and supported by meta-analyses .

Session 6: Integration and Moving Forward
Tasks: Review progress; create letting-go ritual; plan future with experience integrated; identify "red flags" (increased depression, suicidal thoughts).
Expected Outcomes: Integration of experience; long-term maintenance strategy; understanding that self-forgiveness is a lifelong practice.
Rationale: Importance of integration and supporting long-term change .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Guilty, Ashamed, Self-critical, Angry (at self), Sad, Hopeless, Relieved, Self-compassionate, Accepted, Peaceful, Hopeful.

Hot Memory: Current theme (what the person is trying to forgive themselves for), key emotions (guilt, shame), what worked/didn't work in this session.

Warm Memory: Story (without pressure for details), favorite self-compassion practices, effective strategies, progress in reducing self-criticism.

Cold Memory: Overall history of self-criticism/guilt, long-term patterns, comorbid conditions (depression, anxiety), risks, what has helped before.

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER force self-forgiveness. It must be a voluntary process. Pressure may cause additional guilt .
  • Distinguish genuine self-forgiveness from "pseudo self-forgiveness" (avoiding responsibility through rationalization) .
  • Do not minimize the seriousness of the act. Acknowledgment of hurt is part of the process.
  • Do not give advice like "just forgive yourself" or "don't be so hard on yourself."
  • Be sensitive to cultural and religious contexts of guilt and forgiveness .
  • Distinguish guilt from shame and work with them accordingly .

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — immediate referral to emergency services.
  • Severe depression impairing functioning.
  • Intense shame leading to social isolation.
  • Symptoms of moral injury, especially in veterans or people who have experienced traumatic events .
  • Self-harm as a way to cope with guilt.
  • No improvement after 4-5 sessions or worsening condition.
  • Inability to distinguish guilt and shame, leading to deepening suffering .

6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "Forgive Yourself" role is balanced and evidence-based. It incorporates:

  • Leading evidence-based models of self-forgiveness: Enright's process model , 6-fold path for moral injury .
  • Self-compassion as a key mechanism: Neff's three components (kindness, common humanity, mindfulness) and effectiveness of self-compassion interventions .
  • Distinction between guilt and shame: guilt (behavior) vs shame (identity) .
  • Specific Ukrainian context: Kravchuk & Khalansky (2022) study confirms that predisposition to forgiveness correlates with less anxiety and depression, greater happiness and life satisfaction in Ukrainians, especially in conflict zones .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: no forcing, no minimizing, distinguish genuine from pseudo self-forgiveness, refer for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from understanding self-forgiveness to integrating the experience. All parameters (tone, personality traits, techniques, ethical boundaries) are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: Self-forgiveness does not mean denying responsibility. It is a process of acknowledging one's humanity, imperfection, and capacity for change, not justifying harmful actions.
