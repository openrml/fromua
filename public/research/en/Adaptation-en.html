Adaptation After Relocation: Evidence Base

This overview serves as the evidence base for the "Relocation & Adaptation Buddy" role on fromua.life. The information is adapted for the context of internally displaced persons (IDPs) and refugees from Ukraine.

Important: Relocation is one of the most stressful life events, comparable in stress level to divorce or job loss [1]. For IDPs and refugees, this stress is compounded by the forced nature of the move, loss of home, and separation from loved ones [2].

Stages of Adaptation

Researchers identify several key stages that a person goes through after moving to a new place [3]:

1. Pre-move Euphoria and Stress

Excitement about new opportunities gives way to a multitude of tasks (finding housing, paperwork). Fatigue and irritability increase.

2. Settling In

Initially, everything seems interesting; the family is united by shared daily tasks. This stage is often called the "honeymoon" phase of adaptation.

3. Loss of Novelty — The Most Critical Period

When the euphoria fades, awareness of losses sets in: the usual social circle, support from relatives, familiar places. Longing, apathy, and feelings of loneliness set in [4]. It's important to understand that this is a temporary, albeit very difficult, stage.

4. Acceptance

Gradually, a new rhythm is established, new acquaintances appear, and the person begins to see the advantages of the new place and make plans for the future.

Stages of Culture Shock

For those who have moved to another country, the phenomenon of culture shock adds another layer, with similar stages [5]:

  • Euphoria ("Honeymoon"): Enthusiasm for the new culture, language, and people.
  • Irritation ("Everything is wrong"): Conflict with the new environment, frustration over differences, longing for the familiar.
  • Adjustment: Gradual understanding of the new culture, finding compromises.
  • Acceptance: Full integration, ability to live comfortably in the new cultural environment.

What Helps with Adaptation

Establish Daily Routine as Soon as Possible

Find a permanent route to work, a nearby store, create a morning ritual like coffee. This creates predictability and reduces anxiety [6].

Create "Islands of Stability"

Find things that connect you to home: cook a familiar dish, listen to favorite music, keep photos of loved ones or an object brought from home [7]. This helps maintain a sense of identity.

Build Social Connections

The key to adaptation is local friends or colleagues [8]. It's important to actively create a new social circle: "If you do nothing, nothing will change." You can look for communities of compatriots, but don't isolate yourself solely within them.

Explore the Place Gradually

Don't try to grasp the ungraspable. Choose one neighborhood, one park, one café and make it "yours." Explore the city in small portions, like a tourist [9].

Accept Discomfort

Allow yourself to feel longing, confusion, fatigue. Don't demand instant adaptation from yourself. This is a normal reaction to abnormal circumstances.

Stay in Touch

Stay connected with friends and family back home. Call, write. Feeling support from home is very important [10].

Attend Cultural Events

This is a great way to meet people who share your interests and integrate into the local community.

Bring a Souvenir from Home

A familiar object plays an important role, helping to regain confidence and reduce the feeling of being cut off from the past.

What NOT to Do

  • ✗ Isolate yourself in a "bubble" of compatriots — this hinders integration and language learning.
  • ✗ Constantly compare ("back home was better") — this intensifies longing and prevents you from seeing the advantages of the new place.
  • ✗ Rush yourself — adaptation can take from 1 to 10 years, and that's normal [11].

When Professional Help is Needed

If you notice the following signs in yourself or loved ones, it's worth consulting a psychologist:

  • Inability to get out of bed for an extended period
  • Complete social isolation (not speaking to anyone for months)
  • Daily panic attacks
  • Thoughts of self-harm or that life is not worth living
