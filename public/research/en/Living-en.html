Living with Chronic Pain: Evidence Base

This overview serves as the evidence base for the "Chronic Pain Companion" role on fromua.life. The information is adapted for Ukrainians living with chronic pain due to injuries (veterans, civilians), illnesses, and prolonged wartime stress.

Chronic pain, typically defined as pain lasting more than 3 months, affects at least 20% of the adult population worldwide [1]. It is not just a physical sensation but a complex experience affecting all areas of life. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines pain as "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience", emphasizing that it is always a personal experience influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors [2].

Key Findings

🔹 Pain and Emotions Are Inseparable
Research shows that approximately 40% of adults with chronic pain may suffer from anxiety or depression, and up to 80% have some form of mental health issues [3]. Pain and negative emotions form a vicious cycle: pain increases anxiety and irritation, while negative emotions, in turn, intensify the pain experience [4].
🔹 Emotional Regulation as a Key to Relief
Recent studies demonstrate that improving the ability to manage emotions can reduce chronic pain intensity [5]. Therapy focused on emotional regulation (reducing negative emotions and enhancing positive ones) has shown clinically significant pain reduction and improved quality of life [6]. "By changing how we manage emotions, we can change the pain experience itself", researchers note.
🔹 Psychological Resilience Is a Learnable Skill
Psychological resilience to pain is viewed as a dynamic process, not an innate trait. It can be developed through cognitive and emotional strategies such as reappraising the meaning of pain, acceptance, and distancing [7]. Increasing psychological resilience activates natural pain-relief mechanisms in the brain.
🔹 Multifaceted Approach Is Most Effective
The best results come from combining different approaches: medical care, physical therapy, education about the nature of pain, and psychological support [8]. Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), mindfulness, and relaxation techniques have proven effectiveness in reducing pain intensity, catastrophizing, and improving functional status.
🔹 Finding Joy Is Part of Therapy
Learning to notice and enhance positive emotions, even against a background of pain, is an important component of modern approaches to chronic pain treatment [9]. Identifying valued aspects of life and setting goals for the future helps break the "pain-suffering" cycle.
🔹 Pain Catastrophizing Is the Main Enemy
The tendency to exaggerate the threat of pain and think of the worst (catastrophizing) is closely linked to higher pain intensity and disability [10]. Working with catastrophic thoughts is a key element of psychological support.

Practical Techniques for Living with Chronic Pain

🧘 "Stop and Name" Technique
When pain intensifies, pause and ask yourself: "What am I feeling right now?" (pain, anxiety, anger, sadness). Simply naming the emotion reduces its intensity.
🌬️ Breathing into the Emotion
Imagine the emotion (e.g., anger) has a shape, color, temperature. On the inhale, direct air to that place; on the exhale, release the tension. Take 5-10 such breaths.
📝 "Three Good Things" Technique
Every evening, write down three positive moments from the day. It can be something very small: sun outside the window, a tasty coffee, a loved one's smile. This trains the brain to notice good things even against a background of pain.
🧠 Working with Catastrophic Thoughts
When a thought like "this will never end" appears, say "STOP" to yourself. Ask: "Can I know this for sure? What can I do right now to ease my condition?" Replace the thought with a more realistic one: "It hurts now, but there are moments of relief."
🙏 Body Scan with Gratitude
Slowly move your attention through your body, feeling each area. Where there is pain, acknowledge it. Where there is no pain, thank your body. End with the phrase: "My body is doing everything it can to protect me."

Ukrainian Context

Resources for People with Chronic Pain in Ukraine:

  • Medical facilities — neurologists, pain specialists (algologists)
  • Rehabilitation centers — for veterans and civilians
  • Lifeline Ukraine — crisis hotline: 7333
  • Support groups — for people with chronic pain (online)
  • Free psychological help — through NGOs

When Professional Help is Needed

Red Flags:
  • Suicidal thoughts due to pain ("I don't want to live with this pain anymore")
  • Inability to get out of bed due to pain for weeks
  • Loss of all meaning in life because of pain
  • Pain has become unbearable and you don't know what to do
  • Complete social isolation due to pain
