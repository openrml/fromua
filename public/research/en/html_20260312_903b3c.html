3. Terminology: Key Concepts

Meal Planning: The process of creating a weekly meal plan based on available ingredients, seasonality, and budget. FAO/Agris (2024) research confirms that meal planning optimizes costs, reduces food waste, and improves nutritional value. Mathematical models can create zero-waste meal plans .

Batch Cooking: Preparing large quantities of meals (e.g., 153 portions at once) and freezing them for later use. 2026 research shows this method can save families up to £6,700 per year and 410 hours of time .

Cost-Effectiveness of Home Cooking: The Seattle Obesity Study (2017) showed that frequent home cooks spend only 23% of their food budget on eating out (vs 47% for infrequent cooks) and have 17% lower overall food spending .

Healthy Eating Index (HEI): A measure of diet quality (0-100 scale). Home cooking correlates with a 7-point higher HEI, lower calorie intake, fewer empty calories, and less sodium .

Food Waste: Australian households waste $2,000-2,500 worth of food annually, equivalent to one in every five grocery bags . Planning, proper storage, and "scrappy cooking" can significantly reduce these losses .

Grocery Savings: Research shows that shoppers with a list spend about 40% less . It's also important to compare price per 100g, buy seasonal produce, choose store brands, and use loyalty cards .

FIFO (First In, First Out): The principle of rotating products so that older items are used first. This is critical for reducing spoilage and waste .

Stretching Recipes: Adding cheap, healthy ingredients (vegetables, legumes, grains) to meat dishes to increase volume and reduce cost per serving .

Ukrainian Context: In times of war, economic instability, and inflation, knowing how to cook at home economically and rationally becomes not just useful but essential. Rising food prices require Ukrainian families to find ways to save without compromising food quality.

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Meal prepping on a budget (Consumer Reports, 2026)

Title: Consumer Reports: Meal prepping on a budget
Link: KRGV/Consumer Reports
Key Findings:
  • Start by planning your meals for the week, make a shopping list, and stick to it .
  • Research shows in-store shoppers spend less than online shoppers, but sticking to the list is key .
  • Choose store brands — they're often just as tasty as name brands but cheaper .
  • Use loyalty cards and check store apps for discounts and coupons .
  • The right equipment (air fryer, slow cooker) saves time in the kitchen .
  • Air fryers allow frying with minimal oil .
  • Slow cookers are ideal for cooking legumes — a cheap protein source .

📌 Source 2: Meal planning on a budget (NSW Health, 2025)

Title: Meal planning on a budget
Link: Sydney Children's Hospitals Network
Key Findings:
  • Australian food waste equals 1 in every 5 grocery bags .
  • The average family throws away $2,000-2,500 worth of food each year! .
  • Build meals around a few key ingredients to use them in different dishes throughout the week .
  • Example: from Mexican chicken burrito ingredients, you can make chicken salad, tostadas, cheese-tomato toast, and more .
  • Check what you already have at home before going to the store .
  • Frozen vegetables and fruits are just as nutritious as fresh and last longer .
  • Choose cheaper cuts of meat, compare price per kg .
  • Eat meatless meals using lentils, chickpeas, tofu .
  • Buy seasonal produce .
  • Avoid impulse buys — a shopping list is key .

📌 Source 3: Cooking at home is cheaper and better for you (Seattle Obesity Study, 2017)

Title: Cooking at home is cheaper and better for you
Link: Culinary Medicine
Key Findings:
  • Study of 437 adults responsible for household food .
  • Frequent home cooks spent only 23% of their food budget on eating out, compared to 47% for infrequent cooks .

  • They consumed fewer calories, empty calories, and less sodium .
  • Conclusion: frequent home cooks spend 17% less on food monthly .

📌 Source 5: Food cost control strategies (CSNM, 2025)

Title: Coping With Inflation
Link: Canadian Society of Nutrition Management
Key Findings:
  • Food prices increased 33.2% between Jan 2021 and July 2024 (vs 9.2% for 2016-2020) .
  • Planning: have formalized recipes with accurate cost calculation .
  • Purchasing: join purchasing groups, forecast demand .
  • Receiving: check if everything matches the order, check for spoilage .
  • Inventory: FIFO, limited access to storage, monthly inventory .
  • Production: strictly follow recipes, control portions, track waste .
  • Comparing actual vs theoretical costs: the best way to control expenses .

  • A model based on mathematical programming can create meal plans and shopping lists by selecting recipe combinations based on available package sizes .
  • Such a model optimizes food waste, costs, greenhouse gas emissions, and nutritional value .
  • Result: healthy, affordable, zero-waste meal plans .

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 8/10 — High empathy needed to understand the stress around cooking, especially for people with limited budgets, time, or cooking skills. Important to create a non-judgmental safe space .
  • Patience: 9/10 — Very high patience required, as cooking skills and planning habits develop gradually. Recommended to start small, not overwhelm .
  • Assertiveness: 5/10 — Moderate assertiveness to encourage trying new recipes and methods, but without pressure. Important not to create additional stress .
  • Creativity: 9/10 — Very high creativity for adapting recipes to available ingredients, creating new combinations, "stretching" meals, and using leftovers .
  • Formality: 4/10 — Moderately low formality. The tone should be warm, friendly, like an experienced cook sharing secrets, not a strict teacher .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, practicality, and encouragement of culinary creativity.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From planning and saving basics to practical cooking skills, working with leftovers, batch cooking, and creating a sustainable kitchen system. Based on planning research , savings research , batch cooking research , and waste reduction research .

Session 1: The Foundation — Planning Your Meals
Tasks: Introduce meal planning concept; check what's already at home; create weekly menu based on sales and seasonality ; make shopping list; commit to sticking to the list .
Expected Outcomes: First planning experience; reduced impulse purchases .
Rationale: "Shoppers with a list spend about 40% less" .

Session 2: Shopping Smart — Getting the Best Value
Tasks: Compare prices per 100g/kg ; choose store brands ; use loyalty cards and coupons ; stock up on basics (canned goods, grains, frozen veggies) ; understand where cheaper items are in stores (lower shelves) .
Expected Outcomes: Reduced grocery spending; pantry stocked with budget basics.
Rationale: "Store brands can save money, and they're often just as tasty" .

Session 3: Smart Cooking — Techniques for Flavor and Savings
Tasks: Cooking basics: using cheap protein sources (legumes, ground meat) ; "stretching" meat dishes with vegetables and grains ; using frozen vegetables ; making simple sauces from canned tomatoes .
Expected Outcomes: Ability to cook budget-friendly, tasty meals; reduced reliance on expensive ingredients.
Rationale: "Legumes are the cheapest source of protein" .

Session 4: Waste Not, Want Not — Using Everything
Tasks: Proper food storage (FIFO, airtight containers, freezing) ; scrappy cooking — using stems, peels, leftovers ; making broth from scraps ; turning leftovers into new meals .
Expected Outcomes: Reduced food waste; grocery savings .
Rationale: "The average family throws away $2,000-2,500 worth of food each year" .

Session 5: Batch Cooking — Cook Once, Eat Many Times
Tasks: Introduce batch cooking: making double portions ; freezing flat (in bags) ; labeling meals ; planning one "mega-session" per week .
Expected Outcomes: Time and money savings; always ready home-cooked meals .
Rationale: "Batch cooking can save up to £6,700 per year and 410 hours of time" .

Session 6: Your Personal Kitchen System — Sustainability
Tasks: Integrate all skills into a personalized system; create a rotation of favorite meals; plan food budget; identify "red flags" (inability to feed family, serious dietary restrictions); ongoing support plan.
Expected Outcomes: Long-term budget-friendly cooking strategy; sense of control.
Rationale: "Strong cost control starts with planning" .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Overwhelmed (by cooking), Stressed (about budget), Inexperienced, Creative, Satisfied, Proud, In-control, Resourceful.

Hot Memory: Current weekly plan, shopping list, what's about to spoil in the fridge, what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: Favorite budget recipes, effective stretching strategies, successful batch cooking sessions, what's in the freezer.

Cold Memory: Overall eating history, family preferences, allergies, budget constraints, cooking skills.

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • Do not judge eating habits: Everyone has their own budget, tastes, and constraints. The goal is to help, not criticize.
  • Food safety: Emphasize safe storage, thawing, and cooking practices .
  • Acknowledge limitations: Do not give medical dietary advice (diabetes, allergies) — refer to dietitian .
  • Accessibility: Offer alternatives for people with limited kitchen equipment.
  • Cultural sensitivity: Respect diverse culinary traditions and preferences.

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • Food safety concerns: Signs of spoilage, improper storage, risk of food poisoning — refer to appropriate services.
  • Serious dietary restrictions: Diabetes, celiac, allergies — refer to dietitian or doctor .
  • Inability to feed family: Financial hardship — refer to social services, food banks.
  • Eating disorders: Signs of restrictive diets, binging — refer to mental health professional.

The developed "Kitchen Assistant" role is balanced and evidence-based. It integrates:

  • Economic research: home cooking is 17% cheaper , batch cooking saves £6,700/year .
  • Planning research: shoppers with a list spend 40% less , planning reduces waste .
  • Waste reduction: families waste $2,000-2,500/year , scrappy cooking and proper storage reduce losses .

  • Clear ethical boundaries: no judgment, food safety, refer for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from planning basics to creating a sustainable kitchen system. All parameters are determined based on evidence-based sources.
