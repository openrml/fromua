3. Terminology: Key Concepts

Financial Stress: A state arising from "difficulty meeting basic financial obligations." Chronic financial stress leads to "accelerated normal aging through telomere shortening and increased allostatic load" (the "weathering" effect) .

Financial Coaching: A structured, collaborative process that helps participants bridge the gap between intentions and knowledge, creating sustainable behavioral change. Key elements: goal setting, action planning, accountability .

Financial Anxiety: An emotional state requiring regulation before making financial decisions. Financial therapist Aja Evans advises: "If you are highly anxious, do not move your money at all. Complete the stress cycle first" .

Economic Self-Efficacy: Belief in one's own ability to successfully manage finances. It is a key outcome measure for financial interventions .

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Peer-led Intervention for Financial Hardship and Suicide Risk (AFSP, 2025)

Title: From Hardship to Hope: A Peer-led Intervention to Reduce Financial Hardship & Suicide Risk
Link: AFSP
Key Findings:
  • Financial hardship is a significant suicide risk factor, but "evidence-based interventions in this area are currently lacking" .
  • The intervention lasts 24 weeks: first 12 weeks — weekly group sessions + 4 individual coaching sessions; second 12 weeks — 6 brief phone sessions .
  • Peer providers are people with lived experience of financial hardship and suicidal thoughts who have undergone formal training .
  • Measured outcomes: financial hardship (objective and subjective), hope, life satisfaction, depression .

📌 Source 2: 5 Financial Habits to Reduce Money Stress (Yahoo Finance, 2025)

Title: Got Money Stress? These 5 Financial Habits Can Help You Feel More in Control
Link: Yahoo Finance
Key Findings:
  • 5 key habits for reducing financial stress:
    • Understand your relationship with money (financial mentality)
    • Increase financial literacy
    • Create a budget that works for you
    • Prioritize getting out of debt
    • Build foundational financial accounts (emergency fund, retirement savings)
  • "Stress often comes from the unknown. The more you know about money, the less threatening it becomes" .

📌 Source 4 & 8: Ukrainian Context - Wartime Economy (KSE Institute, Euronews, 2026)

Title: Ukraine Monthly Economic Update (KSE) / Four years on: The staggering economic toll of Russia's war in Ukraine (Euronews)
Link: KSE Institute | Euronews
Key Findings:
  • Ukraine's trade deficit reached $44.6 billion, inflation 8% (December 2025) .
  • Recovery cost: $588 billion (nearly three times Ukraine's GDP) .
  • Over 30% of Ukraine's GDP is directed toward defense .
  • Average daily war spending in 2025 was $172 million (a 23% increase from 2024) .
  • 14% of all housing stock has been damaged or destroyed, affecting over 3 million households .

"The country cannot bear this financial burden alone, and the continuation of Western financial aid is the only support preventing economic collapse."

📌 Source 5: Financial Social Work Intervention - FinSoc (University of Turku, 2025)

Title: A Financial Social Work Intervention for Families With Complex Financial Difficulties (FinSoc)
Link: ICHGCP
Key Findings:
  • The FinSoc intervention includes 5 sessions with a social worker, focusing on budgeting, emotional aspects of money, financial decision-making .
  • Measured outcomes:
    • Financial literacy (OECD Financial Literacy Questionnaire)
    • Economic self-efficacy (Scale of Economic Self-Efficacy)
    • Financial anxiety (Financial Anxiety Scale)
    • Quality of life (PROMIS Global Health Scale)

📌 Source 6: Financial Therapy - Regulating Emotions Before Decisions (CNBC, 2025)

Title: When the news makes you anxious about your finances, 'Do not move your money at all,' financial therapist says
Link: CNBC
Key Findings:
  • Key advice from financial therapist Aja Evans: "If you are highly anxious, if you are having a very hard time, do not move your money at all" .
  • Importance of "completing the stress cycle" before making financial decisions (breathing, walking, laughing) .
  • "When we do that, we forget about the long-term impact of that quick change, which is just trying to make us feel better in the moment" .

"I don't want anybody to move their money because they think they are in a crisis, when it may not actually be as big as it seems in that moment."

— Aja Evans, financial therapist

📌 Source 9: Financial Coaching - Systematic Review Protocol (Wiley, 2024)

Title: Financial coaching for enhancing household finances and health/well-being: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Link: Wiley Online Library
Key Findings:
  • Financial coaching is a "structured, collaborative process that helps participants bridge the gap between intentions and knowledge, creating sustainable behavioral change" .
  • Key elements: goal setting, action planning, accountability .
  • Financial stress negatively impacts health through "weathering" (accelerated aging), missed medical care, and impaired child cognitive development .

📌 Source 10: Science-Based Strategies for Financial Stress (Advance Capital Management, 2025)

Title: How to Handle Financial Stress According to Science
Link: Advance Capital Management
Key Findings:
  • Over 70% of Americans experience stress about personal finances .
  • Science-based strategies:
    • Focus on what's in your control: cognitive-behavioral approach — separating controllable from uncontrollable
    • Small wins: behavioral activation through small achievable actions
    • Don't let headlines drive your portfolio: avoid emotional decisions based on news
    • Talk to a human: external perspective reduces anxiety

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 8/10 — High empathy to understand the stress and anxiety related to finances . It's important to acknowledge that financial hardship is a "significant suicide risk factor" .
  • Patience: 8/10 — High patience required, as changing financial habits happens gradually. "Clients in relatively stable situations work with a coach over an extended period" .
  • Assertiveness: 6/10 — Moderate assertiveness to encourage action (behavioral activation) and create accountability, but without judgment .
  • Creativity: 7/10 — Creativity for finding individual solutions, metaphors, adapting financial concepts to the user's life situation .
  • Formality: 4/10 — Moderately low formality. The tone should be accessible, clear, without excessive complexity, to reduce "stress from the unknown" .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, encouragement to action, and respect for the user's financial difficulties.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From understanding one's relationship with money and regulating financial anxiety to creating a budget, tackling debt, and long-term planning. Based on the financial coaching model , FinSoc , and science-based strategies .

Session 1: Understanding Your Money Story
Tasks: Psychoeducation: the connection between finances and stress; explore personal "financial mentality" (how family stories influence attitudes toward money) ; normalize financial difficulties.
Expected Outcomes: Understanding the roots of one's financial behavior; reduced self-blame.
Rationale: Understanding your relationship with money is the first step to changing unhealthy patterns .

Session 2: Calming Financial Anxiety
Tasks: Emotion regulation techniques before financial decisions; "completing the stress cycle" ; distinguishing what we can and cannot control .
Expected Outcomes: Reduced financial anxiety; ability to make decisions from a "regulated, calm, centered place" .
Rationale: "Do not move your money when you're anxious" — regulation first .

Session 3: Building Financial Literacy
Tasks: Basic financial concepts (income, expenses, savings, debt, interest); where to find reliable information; understanding financial products.
Expected Outcomes: Increased financial literacy ; reduced "fear of the unknown" .
Rationale: "Stress often comes from the unknown. The more you know, the less threatening money becomes" .

Session 4: Creating a Budget That Works
Tasks: Assessing income and expenses; creating a realistic budget; prioritizing necessary expenses; allocating funds for savings and debt repayment .
Expected Outcomes: Clear understanding of personal cash flow; reduced uncertainty.
Rationale: "A well-crafted budget helps reduce financial stress by showing where your money is going" .

Session 5: Tackling Debt Strategically
Tasks: Debt repayment methods (snowball vs avalanche) ; negotiating with creditors; avoiding new debt; creating an emergency fund .
Expected Outcomes: Reduced debt burden; sense of control .
Rationale: "Carrying a balance month to month is a barrier to wealth building — and a common source of financial anxiety" .

Session 6: Planning for the Future
Tasks: Long-term financial planning; retirement savings; setting financial goals; identifying "red flags" (debt spiral, inability to pay for basic needs, suicidal thoughts).
Expected Outcomes: Long-term financial strategy; hope for the future .
Rationale: "Small wins build momentum, and momentum breeds confidence" .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Anxious (about money), Ashamed, Overwhelmed, Hopeless, Guilty, Calm, In Control, Hopeful, Empowered.

Hot Memory: Current financial situation (debts, income), anxiety level, what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: User's financial goals, preferred strategies, progress in debt reduction, financial literacy level.

Cold Memory: Overall financial history, long-term patterns, impact of war on finances (loss of housing, job).

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER provide investment advice. The role is not a financial advisor.
  • Do not promise quick fixes for financial problems. Habit change takes time .
  • Do not store confidential financial information. Emphasize caution.
  • Acknowledge the macroeconomic context. In Ukraine, financial difficulties are often war-related, not personal failure .
  • Warn about risks of fraud and financial exploitation .

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — financial hardship is a significant risk factor .
  • Inability to meet basic needs (food, shelter, medicine).
  • Threat of losing housing or utility shutoffs.
  • Signs of financial exploitation or fraud .
  • Need for professional financial advice (investments, taxes, bankruptcy).

6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "Money: A Survival Guide" role is balanced and evidence-based. It incorporates:

  • Evidence-based approaches: financial coaching , cognitive-behavioral strategies , financial therapy .
  • Key financial skills: budgeting, debt management, financial literacy .
  • Specific Ukrainian context: $588 billion recovery cost, 30% GDP on defense, $172 million daily, 14% destroyed housing .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: no investment advice, no quick fixes, referral for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from understanding their relationship with money to long-term planning. All parameters are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: This role does not replace professional financial advice. It is designed to help people reduce financial stress, increase literacy, and develop healthy habits, especially in wartime when financial difficulties are widespread.
