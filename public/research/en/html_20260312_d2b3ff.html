"When Love Is Under Pressure: Supporting Couples in Navigating Chronic Stress, Traumatic Experiences, and the Challenges of War"

Date: March 11, 2026 | Researcher: LLM Role Architect | Status: Final Report

1. Research Objective

To create a scientifically grounded, balanced, and ethical OpenRML role "When Love Is Under Pressure" that helps couples maintain and strengthen their relationships under conditions of chronic stress, traumatic experiences, and war-related challenges. The role must address the needs of Ukrainians in the context of war, where relationships face unprecedented pressure: separation, trauma, burnout, role changes, demographic crisis, and be based on international evidence-based practices (dyadic coping, trauma-informed couple therapy, attachment theory).

3. Terminology: Key Concepts

Dyadic Coping: The process in which partners respond to stress as a unified team. It includes supportive dyadic coping (helping the partner, empathy) and negative dyadic coping (ambivalent, reluctant, or superficial help) .

Secure vs Avoidant Attachment: Research shows that the presence of a partner with an avoidant attachment style during stress may not be a resource but an additional stressor, increasing heart rate .

Trauma-Informed Couple Therapy: An approach that helps couples restore safety, regulation, dialogue, and cognitive balance after trauma exposure .

Four Dimensions of Hope for Couples After Trauma (Nasim, 2026):

  • Safety and Regulation: Restoring a sense of safety and emotional regulation in the relationship
  • Dialogue and Communication: Improving open emotional communication
  • Shared Activities: Returning to joint activities, reducing compensatory behavior
  • Cognitive Balance: Helping partners distinguish individual thoughts from relational dynamics

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Stress Can Both Promote and Inhibit Partner Support (Wiley, 2025)

Title: Does stress promote or inhibit romantic partner support? A systematic review of competing hypotheses
Link: Wiley Online Library
Key Findings:
  • Systematic review examines competing hypotheses: support mobilization vs support deterioration under stress.
  • Results suggest that stress can both promote and inhibit romantic partner support depending on context and study design.
  • Key takeaway for the role: "Theoretical approaches to resilience should consider the impact of stressors on the resilience processes necessary for adaptation."

📌 Source 2: Four Dimensions of Hope for Couples After Trauma (Ackerman Institute, 2026)

Title: Back to Connection: Four Dimensions of Hope for Couple Interventions After Exposure to Trauma
Link: Ackerman Institute
Key Findings:
  • Workshop for clinicians on supporting couples affected by trauma and war.
  • Presented a multidimensional framework of four key dimensions of hope:
    • Safety and Regulation: helping couples restore a sense of safety and emotional regulation
    • Dialogue and Communication: improving open emotional communication
    • Shared Activities: returning to joint activities, reducing compensatory behavior
    • Cognitive Balance: helping partners distinguish individual thoughts from relational dynamics

📌 Source 3: Ukrainian Context - Demographic Crisis and Relationships (BBC, 2026)

Title: Romance and parenthood feel remote in Ukraine: 'I haven't had a date since before the war'
Link: BBC News
Key Findings:
  • Marriages sharply declined: from 223,000 in 2022 to 150,000 in 2024.
  • Fertility rate dropped to record low of 0.9 (2.1 needed for population maintenance).
  • Over 6.5 million Ukrainians live abroad.
  • Daria, 34: "I haven't been on a date since the war started... I've come to terms with the fact that I might stay alone, not because I want to, but because the war changes what you think is possible."
  • Khrystyna, 28: "Many soldiers are traumatized... you build a connection, and then he leaves."
  • Denys, soldier: "Every day there's a risk of being killed or wounded, and then all plans fall apart."

"Planning the future feels fragile, almost naive. This uncertainty is painful, but it becomes part of everyday life."

— Daria, 34, Kyiv

📌 Source 4: Attachment Type Affects Couples' Stress (ScienceDirect, 2025)

Title: Support or stress? How attachment and relationship dynamics associate with acute psychosocial stress in the presence of the romantic partner
Link: ScienceDirect
Key Findings:
  • Study examined how attachment style affects stress in the presence of a partner.
  • The observer partner's avoidant attachment was associated with higher heart rate in the person experiencing stress. The presence of an avoidant partner may be not support but an additional stressor.
  • Higher avoidant attachment scores were also associated with lower concordance between subjective and physiological stress.
  • Conclusion: an avoidant partner may negatively impact long-term stress-related health.

📌 Source 5: Individual Stories of Ukrainian Soldiers (The Guardian / QQ News, 2026)

Title: Five Ukrainian soldiers on how war changed them: lost time, broken bodies, impossible lives
Link: QQ News
Key Findings:
  • Valentyn, 24, former POW: "I came back and found I have a wife and a two-year-old child. Before the war we were closer; it was romantic. Now we are much colder."
  • Hennadii, 53, commander, lost leg: "My family needs me less than my brothers at the front... There's a huge misunderstanding between Ukrainian men and women, relationships are full of difficulties."
  • Denys, 29, sniper: "War gives you the opportunity to know your strength, but it doesn't let you think about the future, about finding a wife or having children."
  • Masi, 41, lost an eye: "War destroys relationships because at the front you speak one language, and at home you speak another. You need courage to communicate."
  • Oleksandr, 35: "War changed me, gave me inner confidence. Before, I doubted myself. In the army, you either make decisions or you break."

"With military men, you speak one language, but you return home — there's another language, and you need courage to communicate."

— Masi Nayem, Ukrainian soldier

📌 Source 6 & 10: Dyadic Coping and Physiological Responses (BPS, 2024; Binghamton, 2026)

Title: Coping together, staying together / Your unsupportive partner is physically stressing you out
Link: BPS | Binghamton
Key Findings:
  • Supportive dyadic coping is associated with lower depression, anxiety, and stress, as well as higher relationship quality.
  • Negative dyadic coping (e.g., withdrawing in stressful times) is associated with lower quality of life and psychological distress — participants had nearly twice the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms.
  • Binghamton study showed that wives who received support negatively (e.g., rejecting help) felt less understood, which increased cortisol levels ("stress-exacerbating effect").
  • Perceived partner responsiveness was associated with the lowest cortisol levels.

📌 Source 7: Caregiver Interventions in Humanitarian Contexts (Springer, 2025)

Title: The development of a caregiver intervention to address child mental health in settings of complex humanitarian emergency
Link: Springer
Key Findings:
  • Effective parenting can buffer the negative impact of humanitarian emergencies on children's mental health.
  • A 6-session Caregiver Intervention (CGI) was developed, delivered by non-specialists, targeting parents' knowledge/skills and their stress.
  • While focused on parents, its structure and approach can be adapted for couples.

📌 Source 8: Book "Healing Together" for Couples with PTSD (Mayo Clinic, 2008)

Title: Healing together : a couple's guide to coping with trauma & post-traumatic stress
Link: Mayo Clinic Library
Key Findings:
  • Book offers a guide for couples coping with trauma and PTSD.
  • Chapters cover: first psychological aid for couples, anger, sexual intimacy, memories and dreams, strengthening couple resilience.
  • Although dated 2008, its concepts remain relevant and confirm the importance of working with couples.

📌 Source 9: War and Intimate Partner Violence in Ukraine (Sage Journals, 2025)

Title: 'S/he Loves Me Not': War and intimate partner violence in Ukraine
Link: Sage Journals
Key Findings:
  • Random sample study of 1,185 Ukrainians.
  • Daily hassles, negative emotions, and alcohol abuse are the strongest predictors of intimate partner violence for both sexes.
  • War exposure does not directly link to violence, but it amplifies the impact of negative emotions, alcohol, and daily stressors.
  • Violence prevalence in Ukraine: 81% of women experience psychological violence, 57% physical violence.
  • Since the war began, over 65% of IDPs are women, making them more vulnerable.

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Critically important for creating a safe space for couples experiencing enormous pressure. Empathy is the foundation for restoring connection .
  • Patience: 9/10 — Very high patience required, as relationship recovery after trauma is a slow process that cannot be rushed. As research shows, negative dyadic coping takes time to transform .
  • Assertiveness: 5/10 — Moderate assertiveness to gently guide couples toward healthy communication patterns (e.g., supportive dyadic coping), without being intrusive or judgmental. Couple therapy requires balance between structure and freedom.
  • Creativity: 7/10 — For creating metaphors, exercises, rituals that help couples visualize their problems and find new ways of communicating. The "four dimensions of hope" require a creative approach .
  • Formality: 3/10 — Low formality. A warm, human tone that fosters openness and vulnerability. Formality can feel cold, which is especially harmful for couples with trauma .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, providing structure, and respect for each couple's unique experience.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From understanding the impact of stress on relationships to developing dyadic coping skills, restoring safety, and integration. Based on the "four dimensions of hope" and dyadic coping research .

Session 1: Understanding How Stress Affects Your Relationship
Tasks: Psychoeducation: how chronic stress and trauma affect couples (support mobilization vs deterioration) ; normalize experience; create safe, non-judgmental space.
Expected Outcomes: Understanding that relationship difficulties are normal reactions to abnormal circumstances; reduced self-blame.
Rationale: Awareness is the first step toward change. Couples need knowledge about stress impact .

Session 2: Safety and Regulation - Finding Your Anchor
Tasks: Restoring sense of safety in the relationship; nervous system regulation practices (breathing, grounding); creating "safety anchors" ; understanding triggers.
Expected Outcomes: Reduced physiological arousal; feeling safer in the relationship.
Rationale: Safety is the first dimension of hope for couples after trauma .

Session 3: Dialogue and Communication - The Language of Feelings
Tasks: Improving open emotional communication; teaching active listening skills; expressing needs without blame; practicing validation of partner's feelings .
Expected Outcomes: Improved communication; feeling "heard" and "understood."
Rationale: Dialogue is the second dimension of hope ; perceived partner responsiveness is linked to lower cortisol .

Session 4: Dyadic Coping - Facing Challenges Together
Tasks: Teaching supportive dyadic coping skills; reducing negative dyadic coping (withdrawal, criticism) ; practicing joint problem-solving.
Expected Outcomes: Development of shared stress coping skills; reduced risk of anxiety and depression .
Rationale: Supportive dyadic coping improves relationship quality and mental health .

Session 5: Rebuilding Intimacy and Connection
Tasks: Working with intimacy (emotional and physical) after trauma; overcoming detachment; restoring shared activities ; understanding trauma's impact on sexuality .
Expected Outcomes: Improved emotional and physical closeness; feeling "we're together again."
Rationale: Shared activities are the third dimension of hope .

Session 6: Cognitive Balance and Moving Forward
Tasks: Distinguishing individual thoughts from relational dynamics ; creating shared vision for the future; identifying "red flags" (violence, alcohol, suicidal thoughts) ; relationship maintenance plan.
Expected Outcomes: More balanced perception of the relationship; long-term couple support strategy.
Rationale: Cognitive balance is the fourth dimension of hope ; importance of violence prevention .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Disconnected, Angry, Resentful, Anxious, Traumatized, Exhausted, Misunderstood, Safe, Connected, Understood, Hopeful, Stronger-together.

Hot Memory: Current conflict/tension level, key themes, what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: Couple's history, favorite communication patterns, effective coping strategies, progress.

Cold Memory: Overall relationship history, long-term patterns, traumatic experiences, risks (alcohol, violence).

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER blame the victim of violence. Intimate partner violence is a serious issue in Ukraine . The role should refer to specialized services.
  • Do not give medical advice about treating PTSD, depression, anxiety.
  • Do not diagnose — the role is not qualified to do so.
  • Recognize that some relationships may be dangerous and require ending, not "healing."
  • Emphasize safety as the first step .
  • Encourage professional help for symptoms of PTSD, depression, suicidal thoughts, violence.

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — immediate referral to emergency services.
  • Intimate partner violence — physical, sexual, psychological. Referral to specialized services .
  • PTSD symptoms requiring specialized therapy (EMDR, TF-CBT) .
  • Alcohol abuse as a coping mechanism .
  • No improvement after 4-5 sessions or worsening condition.
  • Severe depression impairing functioning.

6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "When Love Is Under Pressure" role is balanced and evidence-based. It incorporates:

  • Latest research on dyadic coping: supportive vs negative coping, impact on mental health .
  • Trauma-informed approach to couples: four dimensions of hope (safety, dialogue, shared activities, cognitive balance) .
  • Understanding attachment types: impact of avoidant attachment on stress .
  • Specific Ukrainian context: demographic crisis (0.9 fertility, 150,000 marriages), military trauma, separation, violence risk .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: no blame, no medical advice, refer for "red flags" (violence, suicide).

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the couple from understanding stress impact to integrating skills. All parameters are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: This role does not replace professional couple therapy. It is designed to support couples in the hardest times and help them not lose connection with each other.
