Grief Support: Evidence Base

This overview serves as the evidence base for the "Grief Companion" role on fromua.life. The information is adapted for the Ukrainian context, particularly considering the experience of loss during the war.

What is Grief?

Grief is a natural, multifaceted response to loss, which can be caused by the death of a loved one, the loss of a home, job, relationship, or any other significant life change [1]. It affects emotions, thoughts, behavior, physical health, sense of self, and identity [2].

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross Model: Five Stages of Grief

The most famous model describing the grieving process was developed by psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in 1969 [3]. She identified five stages:

  • Denial: Shock and disbelief that the event occurred ("This can't be happening to me").
  • Anger: Frustration and helplessness, which may be directed at others, oneself, or higher powers ("Why me? It's not fair!").
  • Bargaining: Thoughts of "If only..." or attempts to negotiate with fate ("If I had done things differently...").
  • Depression: Deep sadness, emptiness, fatigue, and loss of interest in life.
  • Acceptance: Not "forgetting" the loss, but learning to live with it and finding new meaning.

It's important to remember:

  • Stages do not necessarily occur sequentially — a person may jump between them, move backward, or experience multiple stages at once [4].
  • Not everyone goes through all stages, and that is also normal.
  • There is no set timeframe for grieving; it is deeply individual [5].

Principles of Supporting a Grieving Person

Supporting the bereaved is based not on special psycho-techniques, but on human presence and acceptance. Key principles include:

  • It requires great emotional tact and genuine human involvement [6].
  • The main thing is to be present, not to "fix" or "cure" the grief.
  • It is important not to avoid talking about the loss and not to emotionally withdraw. Grieving individuals often feel isolated when others avoid the topic of their loss [7].

What NOT to Do: Common Mistakes

  • ✗ Saying: "You should be over it by now", "Time heals all wounds", "Be strong", "Stay strong". This invalidates feelings and sets unrealistic expectations.
  • ✗ Projecting your own experience: "I know exactly how you feel". Every loss is unique.
  • ✗ Rationalizing the loss or looking for positives: "He/she is in a better place now", "You have other children".
  • ✗ Changing the subject when encountering a grieving person to avoid a painful topic.

What Actually Helps

  • ✓ Simply acknowledging feelings as normal: "What you're feeling is normal".
  • ✓ Giving permission to experience any emotion (anger, sadness, relief).
  • ✓ Asking "How are you feeling today?" and being genuinely ready to listen.
  • ✓ Offering concrete help: "I'll make you dinner", "Let me do the grocery shopping".
  • ✓ Encouraging self-care, as grief is physically exhausting [8].

When Professional Help is Needed

Companionship is important, but there are situations where intervention by a mental health professional is necessary [9]:

  • If grief does not ease over time and the intensity of suffering does not diminish (complicated grief).
  • If symptoms of depression persist (constant emptiness, inability to feel joy, sleep and appetite disturbances).
  • If the person cannot maintain a daily routine, work, or perform basic tasks.
  • If there are thoughts of self-harm or that life is not worth living, immediate crisis help is needed.
