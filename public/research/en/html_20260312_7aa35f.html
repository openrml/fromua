🧪 Research for OpenRML Role: Inhabiting a New World

"Inhabiting a New World: When a New City/Country Doesn't Feel Like Home"

Date: March 11, 2026 | Researcher: LLM Role Architect | Status: Final Report

1. Research Objective

To create a scientifically grounded, balanced, and ethical OpenRML role "Inhabiting a New World" that helps people who, due to forced displacement (war, evacuation), find themselves in a new place and struggle to feel "at home." The role integrates research on place attachment formation, nostalgia, adaptation, and integration, with a special focus on Ukrainian refugees and IDPs.

3. Terminology: Key Concepts

Place Attachment: The emotional bond between a person and a specific place. A study of Ukrainian refugees in Poland (N=1,016) revealed a dilemma: strong attachment to the hometown in Ukraine was associated with higher PTSD symptoms and lower life satisfaction. In contrast, attachment to the new place of residence in Poland positively correlated with life satisfaction and posttraumatic growth (PTG) .

Therapeutic Alliance with Refugees: A review of 20 studies (2014-2024) shows unique challenges: cultural and linguistic differences, power dynamics, mistrust due to trauma. The presence of an interpreter transforms the alliance into triadic relationships. Recommendations include flexible therapeutic boundaries, integration of advocacy, continuity of care .

Acculturative Stress: Immigrants face challenges: preserving/changing cultural identity, value conflicts, language barrier, housing, employment. Typical reactions: distress, homesickness, depression, psychosomatic complaints, social maladjustment .

Three Proximities: Research in Finland identifies spatio-temporal, affective, and positional proximity. They are intertwined and influence feelings of safety and comfort. It is important to create "places of joy" where displaced persons can feel belonging .

Belonging on the Move: Belonging is formed in movement between places. It includes memories of the past, daily routines in the new country, and expectations of future transnational connections .

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Place attachment and well-being after forced displacement (Wiley, 2025)

Title: Place cure or place curse? Place attachment and well-being after forced displacement
Link: Wiley Online Library
Key Findings:
  • Study of Ukrainian refugees in Poland (N=1,016).
  • Strong attachment to the hometown in Ukraine was associated with higher PTSD symptoms and lower life satisfaction.
  • Attachment to the new place of residence in Poland was positively associated with life satisfaction and posttraumatic growth (PTG).
  • Old place attachment negatively correlated with new place attachment.
  • The more similar the two places were perceived, the stronger the attachment to the new place.
  • Place attachment can be both a resource and a risk factor.

📌 Source 2: Acculturative stress and homesickness (Cambridge University Press, 2006)

Title: Individual Differences in Acculturative Stress Reactions: Determinants of Homesickness and Psychosocial Maladjustment
Link: Cambridge Core
Key Findings:
  • Immigrants face challenges: preserving/changing cultural identity, value conflicts, housing, language, employment.
  • Typical reactions to acculturative stress: distress, homesickness, depressive reactions (with alienation and hopelessness), psychosomatic complaints, psychosocial maladjustment.
  • Homesickness is a common reaction, closely tied to acculturation processes.

📌 Source 3: Proximities and place attachment in forced migrants' lives (Wiley, 2026)

Title: Traversing Terrains of Proximities and Place Attachment in Forced Migrants' Lives in Finland
Link: Wiley Online Library
Key Findings:
  • Study of 10 participants (walking interviews).
  • Three types of proximity shape place attachment: spatio-temporal, affective, positional.
  • These three types are closely intertwined and influence how safe, comfortable, and close a place feels.
  • It is necessary to create "places of joy" where forced migrants can feel belonging and control over their own lives.

📌 Source 4: Mobile Centres for refugee support (European Commission, 2026)

Title: Mobile Centres delivering integrated support in rural and underserved areas
Link: Europa
Key Findings:
  • Mobile centers for Ukrainian refugees in rural areas of Europe.
  • Location is a critical factor in integration. Lack of access to services exacerbates social isolation, delays labor market integration, and worsens psycho-emotional state.
  • Integrated support includes: language learning, employment counseling, psychosocial support, community engagement.

📌 Source 5: Therapeutic alliance in refugee MHPSS (PubMed, 2025)

Title: The Therapeutic Alliance in Refugee Mental Health and Psychosocial Support: A Narrative Review
Link: PubMed
Key Findings:
  • Review of 20 studies (2014-2024).
  • Unique challenges when working with refugees: cultural and linguistic differences, power dynamics, mistrust due to trauma.
  • The presence of an interpreter transforms the dyadic alliance into a triadic one, creating both advantages and complexities.
  • Recommendations for strengthening the alliance: flexible therapeutic boundaries, integration of advocacy, continuity of care, specialized training for providers and interpreters.

📌 Source 6: WHO on Ukraine's health response (WHO, 2024)

Title: 1000 days of war in Ukraine: resilience in health response, recovery and reform efforts
Link: WHO Europe
Key Findings:
  • 6 million Ukrainian refugees abroad, 3.5 million IDPs within the country.
  • The war has increased mental health needs, trauma therapy.
  • Investments in resilient health facilities and community infrastructure help residents stay in their communities, promoting stability.

📌 Source 7: Ukrainian refugees' adaptation (Biblioteka Nauki, 2025)

Title: Ukrainian refugees' adaptation to and integration with new societies and communities in the context of migration policy management
Link: Biblioteka Nauki
Key Findings:
  • Study of Ukrainian refugees (December 2022 - March 2023).
  • Need for a holistic approach to migration policy that considers the interrelationship of financial, social, educational, and psychological factors.
  • Recommendations include accelerating financial aid programs, educational initiatives, business support.

📌 Source 9: Belonging on the Move (DOAJ, 2025)

Title: Belonging on the Move: A Multi-Method Exploration of Embodied Affect, Place Attachment, and Transnational Mobility in Migrant Lives in Norway
Link: DOAJ
Key Findings:
  • Belonging develops through the interaction of physical environment, personal and family relationships, social and cultural context.
  • Migrants negotiate belonging "on the move" — through memories of the past, daily routines in the new country, and expectations of future transnational connections.
  • Movement and place attachment coexist and constantly shape each other.

📌 Source 10: Social innovation for Ukrainian refugees (ESN, 2026)

Title: Social innovation guided by community care and co-production – the ECSILI pilot
Link: European Social Network
Key Findings:
  • ECSILI project in Warsaw and Arad for the integration of Ukrainian refugees.
  • Co-production principle: involving affected people in all stages — from creation to evaluation of services.
  • Individual plans in Warsaw consider not only needs but also refugees' wishes (e.g., courses for skill enhancement, psychosocial support).
  • In Arad, refugees identified language courses as the primary need.

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Critically important for creating a safe space for people who have experienced forced displacement. Research emphasizes the importance of the therapeutic alliance, complicated by cultural barriers and trauma . Maximum empathy is the foundation.
  • Patience: 9/10 — Very high patience required, as forming new place attachment is a long-term process requiring "inhabiting" new space through daily routines .
  • Assertiveness: 4/10 — Low assertiveness. Cannot push or force to "love" the new place. The role must be a companion who supports, not a director .
  • Creativity: 8/10 — High creativity for helping find "similarities" between old and new places , creating new routines, rituals, "places of joy" , connecting memories with the present .
  • Formality: 3/10 — Low formality. The tone should be warm, human, non-judgmental, considering that many refugees face bureaucracy and alienation .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between deep empathy, patient companionship, and respect for the unique pace of "inhabiting" a new world.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From acknowledging loss and grief for the old place to gradually "inhabiting" the new through embodied experience, routines, connections, and integration of past and present. Based on place attachment research , types of proximity , and belonging on the move .

Session 1: Honoring What Was Lost
Tasks: Create safe space; acknowledge pain of losing home, people, identity; normalize grief and homesickness ; explain that strong old place attachment can be painful, but it's normal .
Expected Outcomes: Feeling that their pain is acknowledged; reduced self-blame for "not being able to forget the old place."
Rationale: "Place attachment can be both a resource and a risk factor" . First, the pain must be acknowledged.

Session 2: Finding Familiarity in the New
Tasks: Explore what in the new place might remind them of the old (architecture, nature, smells, sounds); use the idea of "perceived similarity" as a factor that facilitates attachment ; create a "corner of home" in the new space.
Expected Outcomes: Identification of 2-3 elements of similarity; feeling that the new place is not so foreign.
Rationale: "The more similar the two places are perceived, the stronger the attachment to the new place" .

Session 3: Embodied Presence — Walking the New Ground
Tasks: Explore spatio-temporal and affective proximity through embodied experience ; practice mindful walks in the new neighborhood; pay attention to sensations (sun, wind, sounds); create new "embodied" markers of safety.
Expected Outcomes: Feeling more present in body and space; reduced sense of "disconnectedness."
Rationale: "Belonging develops through embodied affect — sensory and emotional experience from daily routines and interaction with place" .

Session 4: Small Routines, Small Roots
Tasks: Identify and create new daily routines: favorite café, morning walk, library, market. This creates "positional proximity" — a sense of control and predictability .
Expected Outcomes: List of small routines; feeling that the new place is beginning to "respond."
Rationale: "Daily routines in the new country" shape belonging .

Session 5: Weaving Connections
Tasks: Explore social connections (other displaced persons, locals, volunteers); participate in community activities (Mobile Centres, ECSILI) ; overcoming language barriers; working with interpreters as part of the "triadic alliance" .
Expected Outcomes: Identification of 1-2 social contacts; plan to participate in an activity.
Rationale: "Belonging develops through interaction with personal and family relationships, social and cultural context" .

Session 6: Living Between Worlds — Integration
Tasks: Integrate past and present: create a "memory ritual" for the old home and simultaneously plan the future in the new place; recognize that belonging can be "on the move" ; identify "red flags" (depression, isolation, suicidal thoughts).
Expected Outcomes: Feeling of wholeness (no need to "choose" between old and new); long-term plan; knowledge of help resources.
Rationale: "Migrants negotiate belonging through memories of the past, daily routines in the new country, and expectations of future transnational connections" .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Homesick, Lost, Displaced, Grieving, Anxious, Isolated, Curious, Grounded, Connected, Familiar, At-home (however slightly).

Hot Memory: Current level of nostalgia/alienation, triggers (news from home), what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: Elements of similarity between old and new, favorite routines, created "home corners," social contacts, progress.

Cold Memory: History of losses, long-term adaptation patterns, cultural background, past displacement experiences.

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER minimize the pain of losing home. "Old place attachment" can be a deep source of suffering .
  • Do not force to "love" the new place. This is a natural process that cannot be rushed.
  • Acknowledge structural barriers: bureaucracy, language barrier, access to services .
  • Respect cultural differences and the role of the interpreter .
  • Encourage participation in co-production of services — this restores agency .

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — immediate referral to emergency services.
  • Symptoms of clinical depression or PTSD requiring treatment .
  • Social isolation, inability to leave home.
  • Lack of basic needs (housing, food) — referral to social services .
  • Legal issues (refugee status, documents).

It draws on research on place attachment, acculturative stress, and refugee mental health. It is NOT a substitute for professional mental health treatment, therapy, or legal advice. 6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "Inhabiting a New World" role is deeply ethical and evidence-based. It integrates:

  • Latest place attachment research: understanding the "curse" of old attachment and "healing" through new attachment .
  • Concept of three types of proximity: spatio-temporal, affective, and positional .
  • Understanding of belonging on the move: combining memories, present routines, and future connections .
  • Principles of integrated support: mobile centers, co-production of services .
  • Ukrainian context: mass forced migration (6 million refugees, 3.5 million IDPs) and specific adaptation challenges .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: do not minimize loss, do not force to "love" the new, refer for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from acknowledging loss to integrating past and present in a new "world." All parameters are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: This role does not replace professional psychological help. It is designed to be a compassionate companion in the long process of "inhabiting" a new world after losing home.
