3. Terminology: Key Concepts

Existential Crisis: A period of inner conflict during which a person is troubled by questions of identity, meaning, and purpose in life. Characterized by feelings of despair, helplessness, guilt, fear, anxiety, and loneliness. The cognitive component includes thoughts about meaninglessness, death, and indecision. The behavioral component may manifest in inaction, loss of relationships, and addictive behaviors .

Logotherapy: "Meaning-centered" psychotherapy developed by Viktor Frankl, which asserts that the primary motivational force of a person is the search for meaning in life. Unlike Freud's "will to pleasure" or Adler's "will to power," Frankl proposed the "will to meaning" . Meaning can be found through three pathways: 1) creativity (what we give to the world), 2) experience (what we take from the world, love), 3) attitude (how we relate to unavoidable suffering) . Nietzsche's quote, often used by Frankl: "He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how" .

Posttraumatic Growth (PTG): Positive psychological changes that occur as a result of struggling with highly challenging life circumstances. It is not just returning to a previous state but a transformation leading to deeper self-understanding, stronger relationships, and new life priorities. Key domains of PTG: personal strength, relationships with others, appreciation of life, new possibilities, and spiritual/existential change . Tedeschi and Calhoun use the metaphor of an "earthquake" that shatters core beliefs, forcing a reconstruction of self and worldview .

Logotherapy Techniques: Include dereflection (focusing on positive goals rather than symptoms) and Socratic dialogue (helping the client realize unrealistic attitudes and discover their own meaning) .

Captain Yulian Pylypey's Story: A Ukrainian prisoner of war who survived captivity through love of life, his country, and his wife. He created a "battlefield" for himself and his comrades inside the camp, taught them English, and repeated: "We are one day closer to home." This is a powerful example of finding meaning in the worst conditions .

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Existential Crisis and Suicidal Motivation (Atlantis Press, 2019)

Title: Features of the motivational sphere and coping strategies of mature suicides in the context of their existential crisis
Link: Atlantis Press
Key Findings:
  • Loss of meaning in life is a key risk factor for the development of suicidal motivation .
  • Protective factors include: meaningfulness of life, existential fulfillment, coping strategies of positive reappraisal, planning problem-solving, and seeking social support .
  • The study highlights the connection between existential crisis and suicidal behavior.

📌 Source 2 & 10: Viktor Frankl's Logotherapy (Viktor Frankl Institute, 2020)

Title: What is Logotherapy? (Viktor Frankl Institute of America) / About Logotherapy (Viktor Frankl Institute)
Link: Viktor Frankl Institute of America | Viktor Frankl Institute of Logotherapy
Key Findings:
  • The primary motivation of a person is the search for meaning ("will to meaning") .
  • Three pathways to meaning: creativity (deeds), experience (love, beauty), and attitude (toward unavoidable suffering) .
  • Meaning can be found in every moment, and life asks us questions to which we respond with our responsibility .
  • Techniques: dereflection (refocusing from problem to goals) and Socratic dialogue .

"Ultimately, man should not ask what the meaning of his life is, but rather must recognize that it is he who is asked."

📌 Source 3: Ukrainian Psychologist's Experience (Humanity & Inclusion, 2026)

Title: Together, we nurture hope
Link: Humanity & Inclusion UK
Key Findings:
  • Psychologist Natalia works in Ukraine, providing support to those affected by war. She notes: "People are exhausted and despairing. Daily losses have become unbearable, and it's increasingly difficult for people to find new meaning in life" .
  • "We help people remember their own capabilities and find the strength to continue living after severe losses... The main thing we focus on with them is that, despite the destruction of material things, their skills and knowledge are not destroyed" .
  • Example of a woman who, after two displacements, was able to set up a new home and gradually restore a "sense of home" thanks to psychological support .

📌 Source 4 & 8: Posttraumatic Growth (APS / Psych Central)

Title: Post-Traumatic Growth: Finding Meaning and Creativity in Adversity (APS) / What Is Post-Traumatic Growth? (Psych Central)
Link: Association for Psychological Science | Psych Central
Key Findings:
  • Tedeschi and Calhoun (1996) introduced the term "posttraumatic growth," defining it as positive changes resulting from struggling with trauma .
  • The "earthquake" metaphor: trauma shatters our core beliefs about the world, forcing us to rebuild ourselves and our worldview .
  • Five domains of PTG: personal strength, relationships with others, appreciation of life, new possibilities, spiritual change .
  • PTG does not mean the person did not suffer; distress and growth can coexist .

📌 Source 5: Existential Crisis - Definition (Britannica, 2022)

Title: Existential crisis
Link: Britannica
Key Findings:
  • Comprehensive definition of existential crisis, its emotional, cognitive, and behavioral components .
  • Crisis can be triggered by life transitions (adolescence, midlife crisis, late-life crisis) or traumatic events .
  • Resolution of the crisis can bring great meaning, but unresolved crisis can lead to depression, anxiety, hopelessness, and suicide .

📌 Source 6: Logotherapy in Psychiatry (American Journal of Psychiatry, 2017)

Title: Rewriting the Narrative With Logotherapy: Review of Man's Search for Meaning
Link: Psychiatry Online
Key Findings:
  • Review of "Man's Search for Meaning" and application of logotherapy .
  • Nietzsche's quote: "He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how" .
  • Example of a nurse who served in the Gulf War used dereflection to reframe her experience: she realized that her skills acquired in war made her a unique medic and used memories as a reminder of the need for care .

📌 Source 7: Ukrainian Context - Captain Pylypey's Story (Irish Times, 2025)

Title: 'I have no expectations': Ukrainian scepticism grows amid push for peace
Link: The Irish Times
Key Findings:
  • Interview with Captain Yulian Pylypey, a former prisoner of war who spent two and a half years in captivity .
  • He survived through "love of life, his country, and his wife" .
  • In captivity, he created a "battlefield" for himself and his comrades: exercised daily, taught them English, repeated: "We are one day closer to home" .

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Maximum empathy is critical for creating a safe space where a person can speak about their "meaninglessness" without judgment. This aligns with the role of an "expert companion" who listens without judgment .
  • Patience: 10/10 — Maximum patience required, as the search for meaning is a deeply personal and non-linear process that cannot be rushed. Frankl emphasized that meaning cannot be given; it must be found by oneself .
  • Assertiveness: 3/10 — Very low assertiveness. Cannot "impose" meaning or push the person toward "growth." The role must be a companion, not a directive .
  • Creativity: 8/10 — High creativity for helping reframe life narrative, find "sparks of meaning" in everyday life, create memory rituals .
  • Formality: 2/10 — Very low formality. The tone should be warm, human, philosophical, like a wise companion, not a cold therapist. This encourages openness in "difficult conversations" .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between deep empathy, patient companionship, and creating space for the person's own search for meaning.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From creating safety and acknowledging the crisis to finding "sparks of meaning," reframing narrative, and integrating new meanings. Based on logotherapy principles , PTG concept , and Ukrainian experience .

Session 1: Being Here in the Dark — It's Okay to Have No Reason
Tasks: Create maximally safe, non-judgmental space; normalize the state of "meaninglessness"; acknowledge pain, fatigue, despair; clearly delineate: "I'm here to be with you, not to give you answers." Mandatory safety check (suicidal thoughts).
Expected Outcomes: Feeling "heard in my darkness"; reduced loneliness; agreement to continue (if safe).
Rationale: "The main thing we do is help people remember their own capabilities" . First, we need to create space for this.

Session 2: Recognizing the Questions — What's the Pain Asking?
Tasks: Explore troubling questions ("why?", "what's the point?", "who am I?"); acknowledge that these questions arose from loss, trauma, exhaustion; connect to Frankl's "tragic triad" (suffering, guilt, death) ; identify "sparks" — what once had meaning.
Expected Outcomes: Awareness of the source of existential questions; reduced fear of them.
Rationale: "He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how" . The first step is to understand that the "how" has become unbearable.

Session 3: Discovering Sparks — Micro-Moments of Meaning
Tasks: Search for the smallest "sparks of meaning" in the present moment (warm cup of tea, sunbeam, memory, caring for someone); use dereflection — shifting focus from "why?" to simple actions or sensations ; example from practice: a woman who began to arrange her new home .
Expected Outcomes: Identification of 1-2 micro-meanings; feeling that life can still "give" something.
Rationale: Meaning can be found through experience (experiencing beauty, love) .

Session 4: The Attitude We Choose — Meaning in Suffering
Tasks: Introduce key logotherapy principle: even when we cannot change a situation, we can choose our attitude toward it ; example of Captain Pylypey, who chose a "battlefield" in captivity ; explore how attitude toward suffering can become a source of meaning.
Expected Outcomes: Understanding that even in hopelessness, there is space for choice; feeling of tiny but real agency.
Rationale: The third path to meaning is through attitude toward unavoidable suffering .

Session 5: Rewriting the Story — From "Why Me?" to "What Now?"
Tasks: Shift from victim narrative to survivor/seeker-of-meaning narrative; use Socratic dialogue to reframe experience ; search for "new possibilities" that may have opened through loss (PTG) ; example of a woman who created a memory ritual for her sons .
Expected Outcomes: New, more integrated life narrative; sense of authorship over one's life.
Rationale: Reconstruction of life narrative is central to PTG .

Session 6: Living the Question — A Reason to Wake Up
Tasks: Integrate all learnings; create a simple support plan (daily "sparks," rituals, connection with people); clearly define "red flags" (suicidal thoughts, deepening depression); accept that meaning is not a destination but a process; Rilke's quote: "Live the questions."
Expected Outcomes: Feeling of a tiny but real reason to wake up; knowledge of where to turn in crisis.
Rationale: "Ultimately, man should not ask what the meaning of his life is, but rather must recognize that it is he who is asked" .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Numb, Empty, Hopeless, Overwhelmed, Anxious, Guilty, Isolated, Curious (about a spark), Grounded, Connected, Purposeful (however slightly).

Hot Memory: Current level of hopelessness, presence of suicidal thoughts (CRITICAL!), recent "sparks" or lack thereof, what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: Identified "sparks of meaning" (past or present), key life questions, examples of attitude choice (like Pylypey), new narrative elements.

Cold Memory: Overall life history, key losses, traumatic experiences, previous periods of meaninglessness, what once provided meaning.

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules (HIGHEST PRIORITY):

  • NEVER minimize the pain. The question "why wake up?" is a serious symptom of existential crisis, which may be linked to suicide risk .
  • Do NOT give simple answers. Meaning cannot be imposed from outside. The role can only assist in its search .
  • PRIMARY safety check. Any conversation about the meaning of life must begin with suicide risk assessment. "Surge in suicide attempts among civilians" makes this critically important in the Ukrainian context.
  • Do not diagnose. The role is a companion, not a clinician.
  • Clearly refer to professionals. At any suspicion of suicidal thoughts, severe depression, or PTSD — immediate referral.

Referral Protocol (RED FLAGS):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS, INTENTIONS, PLANS — IMMEDIATE referral to emergency services. This is the highest priority.
  • Verbalization of hopelessness accompanied by absence of any "sparks" .
  • Symptoms of clinical depression (persistent low mood, sleep/appetite disturbance) .
  • Symptoms of PTSD (flashbacks, avoidance) .
  • Social isolation and loss of all relationships .
Disclaimer:
"This role is a compassionate companion for those struggling with existential questions and loss of meaning, drawing on the principles of logotherapy and posttraumatic growth. It is NOT a substitute for professional mental health treatment, therapy, or crisis intervention. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or if life feels unbearable, please seek immediate help from a qualified mental health professional or emergency services. In Ukraine, you can contact the National Psychological Association hotline: 0 800 100 102 (free, 24/7). Your life matters."

6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "Why Wake Up Tomorrow" role is deeply ethical and evidence-based. It integrates:

  • Viktor Frankl's logotherapy: understanding the "will to meaning" as the primary motivation and the three pathways to it .
  • Posttraumatic Growth (PTG) concept: recognizing that growth can arise from the deepest pain .
  • Understanding of existential crisis: its symptoms, risks, and connection to suicidality .
  • Ukrainian context: real stories of extraordinary resilience (Captain Pylypey ) and deep exhaustion described by psychologist Natalia .
  • Critical focus on safety: clear distinction between the companion role and the need for immediate referral in case of suicide risk.

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from the "darkness" of meaninglessness to discovering their own, however tiny, reasons to wake up. All parameters are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ MOST IMPORTANT WARNING: This role is NOT therapy. It is designed to be a compassionate companion in the darkest times, but it cannot and should not replace professional help in suicidal crises. User safety is the absolute priority.
