Basic Digital Security in Today's Threat Landscape: Evidence Base

This overview serves as the evidence base for the "Digital Safety & Privacy Guide" role on fromua.life. The information is adapted for Ukrainians in wartime conditions — increased risks of cyber espionage, war-related phishing attacks, secure communications.

In today's world, where more and more aspects of daily life move online, protecting personal information becomes a key task. Cybercriminals target this data, but it doesn't take much effort to strengthen your digital defense. Being digitally secure means having intentional control over your digital life to reduce risks while safely living, working, and communicating online [1].

Key Digital Security Strategies

🔹 Strong Access Protection: Unique Passwords and Password Managers
Passwords are the first line of defense for your online accounts. Using names, birth dates, or simple sequences like "123456" provides almost no protection. Even riskier is using the same password for different accounts: if one service is breached, attackers gain access to others. Therefore, it's important to use a unique, long (at least 16 characters), and complex password for each account, containing uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers, and special symbols [2]. A password manager helps generate and securely store such passwords in an encrypted vault. Crucially, the master password for the manager itself must be extremely strong.
🔹 Two-Factor Authentication (2FA/MFA) as an Extra Lock
This is a second layer of protection, critical because even the strongest password can be stolen. Two-factor authentication acts as an additional lock: even if attackers obtain your password, they still need a second factor to log in (e.g., a code from an app on your phone, a biometric scan, or a physical key). It should be enabled wherever possible, especially for email, bank accounts, and social media [3].
🔹 Caution on Social Media and Data Minimization
Personal data is a valuable resource, particularly for attackers. They often use publicly shared information (like birth dates, pet names, or workplaces) to create personalized phishing messages or social engineering attacks [4]. It's important to regularly review and strengthen privacy settings, and before posting, ask yourself: who will see this, is it really necessary to publish, and how could it be misused? Also, check if photos reveal confidential information (computer screens, sticky notes with passwords).
🔹 Recognizing Phishing and Fraud
Phishing remains the most common online threat. Scammers send fake emails, text messages (smishing), or make phone calls (vishing) to trick you into revealing confidential information or clicking malicious links. Such messages typically create a sense of urgency, fear, or even excitement, so you click before you think [5]. Be extremely cautious with unexpected messages, especially those demanding immediate action or personal information. Never click suspicious links, and when in doubt, contact the organization through official channels.
🔹 Software Updates
Software updates don't just add new features; they also patch security vulnerabilities that attackers exploit [6]. Enable automatic updates for operating systems, browsers, and apps to avoid missing critical fixes. Don't ignore update reminders.
🔹 Data Backup (The 3-2-1 Rule)
The best way to protect valuable work, photos, and other data is to create copies and store them safely. This allows you to recover information in case of a ransomware attack, device failure, or loss. The 3-2-1 rule is recommended: have at least three copies of your data, store them on two different media (e.g., cloud and external drive), and have one copy off-site (away from home or office) [7].
🔹 Safe Use of Public Wi-Fi
Public Wi-Fi in cafes or airports can be dangerous, as traffic can be intercepted. Avoid accessing sensitive accounts (banking, email) over such networks, or use a VPN to encrypt your connection [8]. Also, turn off automatic Wi-Fi and Bluetooth connections on your devices so they don't automatically connect to unknown networks.

Ukrainian Context: Additional Wartime Risks

Specific Threats and Recommendations:

  • War-related phishing campaigns: scammers may send emails supposedly from military units, volunteer funds, or government agencies
  • Communication security: use Signal instead of regular SMS and phone calls
  • Starlink: when using Starlink, ensure it's official equipment and use a strong password for your network
  • Information verification: trust only official sources, verify information before sharing
  • Personal data protection: avoid posting geolocation, photos of military personnel, equipment, or blank documents

When Professional Help is Needed

Red Flags:
  • You've been a victim of financial fraud, lost money
  • Your account was hacked and you can't recover access
  • You're being blackmailed with intimate photos or data
  • You're receiving threats online
  • You don't know if it's safe to communicate now, and you fear surveillance
