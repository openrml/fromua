1. Research Objective

To create a scientifically grounded, balanced, and ethical OpenRML role "You There, Me Here – We Are Together" that helps couples separated by war maintain emotional connection through technology. The role must address the needs of Ukrainians in the context of war, where millions of families are separated due to hostilities and forced migration, and be based on international evidence-based practices (attachment theory, media multiplexity, mixed-media relationships, dyadic coping).

3. Terminology: Key Concepts

Mixed-Media Relationships: Relationships maintained through a combination of different media, including face-to-face communication. In wartime, when face-to-face communication is impossible, couples rely exclusively on technology .

Media Multiplexity: The use of multiple communication channels (phone, video calls, messengers, social media) to maintain relationships. A greater number of channels is associated with stronger ties .

A.R.E. Framework (Johnson): A framework for emotional safety in relationships: Accessibility (is the partner available?), Responsiveness (will they respond to my bid?), Engagement (do they value me?) .

Communication Interdependence: The idea that different communication channels do not exist in isolation but influence each other. For example, a video call can restore emotional connection lost through dry text messages .

Communication Changes in Wartime: Psychologist Natalia Umerenkova notes: "Your brain switches into survival mode to switch off emotions unrelated to the war. Many wives say that communication with their husbands changes — they communicate in the same way as in the military group: short, emotionless messages. And wives ask: 'Is everything okay with us?' They see this as rejection." .

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 2: Impact of War on Ukrainian Families (The Guardian, 2025)

Title: 'If things were bad before, they will be worse': can families recover from the stress and strain of war?
Link: The Guardian
Key Findings:
  • King's College London research: in families where one member serves 12 months over 3 years, relationship problems are 8% more common.
  • Psychologist Natalia Umerenkova (Institute for Social and Political Psychology of Ukraine) identifies three categories of families: (1) those that were close and maintained connection; (2) those that had problems — war exacerbates them; (3) those with different views — war can either bring them together or drive them apart.
  • Key quote on communication: "Your brain switches into survival mode to switch off emotions unrelated to the war. Many wives say that communication with their husbands changes because they communicate in the same way as in the military group: short, emotionless messages. And wives ask: 'Is everything okay with us?' They see this as rejection."
  • Maryna, 41: "I couldn't understand if my emotional reactions were right. At the beginning, I thought the war would last a year at most. Then life would be the same, but it's not."

"When you don't have enough strength to solve problems that arise, to talk about them, it becomes a vicious circle."

— Natalia Umerenkova, psychologist

📌 Source 4: Sustaining Romantic Relationships in Technology-Mediated Communication (Tampere University, 2025)

Title: Sustaining Romantic Relationships in Technology-Mediated Communication: An Attachment-Informed Approach
Link: Trepo
Key Findings:
  • Master's thesis by Jing Yu (2025) examines sustaining romantic relationships in technology-mediated communication (TMC).
  • Uncertainty identified as the main problem in TMC due to limited non-verbal cues and feedback delays, affecting emotional connection.
  • Accessibility, Responsiveness, and Engagement (A.R.E. Framework) remain core needs for intimate relationships, but they vary significantly between individuals.
  • People can reduce uncertainty and strengthen relationship stability through proactive and consistent efforts, flexible use of communication channels, and attentiveness to individual needs.
  • The study extends attachment theory by applying the A.R.E. framework to digital relationships.

📌 Source 5: Impact of Crisis Events on the Family (ScienceDirect, 2025)

Title: Impact of crisis events on the family and technologies of psychological support to overcome the consequences
Link: ScienceDirect
Key Findings:
  • Study on the impact of war in Ukraine on family dynamics.
  • 40% of respondents stated that their families were forced to separate — some stayed in the homeland, others left.
  • Significant changes in family dynamics were found: role confusion due to job loss or injury, emotional disconnection due to prolonged separation, increased risk of relationship breakdown.
  • PTSD identified as a central challenge affecting communication patterns, emotional availability, and behavior in families.
  • Systematized effective technologies for recovery: individual and family therapy, online counseling, digital self-help tools.

📌 Source 6: Couple Relationship Education (Wiley, 2021)

Title: Helping couples achieve relationship success: A decade of progress in couple relationship education research and practice, 2010–2019
Link: Wiley Online Library
Key Findings:
  • Review of 34 studies of Couple Relationship Education (CRE) programs.
  • CRE programs are based on three key pillars of relationship health:
    • Managing negative emotions during conflict (behavioral skills, "time-out")
    • Enhancing positive connections (friendship, sensuality)
    • Strengthening commitment (relationship priority, shared vision of the future)
  • CRE has positive effects not only on relationship quality but also on physical and mental health, co-parenting, and even child well-being.

📌 Source 9: Relationship Maintenance in the Age of Technology (University of Illinois, 2020)

Title: Relationship Maintenance in the Age of Technology
Link: Illinois Experts
Key Findings:
  • Contemporary maintenance of romantic relationships occurs in "mixed-media relationships" — through multiple media, including face-to-face communication.
  • Technology can both help maintain relationships and create additional burden and problems.
  • How people use technology influences its effect on relationships.
  • Despite the growing role of technology, face-to-face communication remains important for relationship maintenance.

📌 Source 10: Impact of War on Children in Ukraine (NZ Herald / World Vision, 2026)

Title: Four winters of war - what Ukraine's children are living through
Link: NZ Herald
Key Findings:
  • Arman Grigoryan (World Vision) on the impact of war on children.
  • 50% of children aged 13-15 have persistent sleep disturbances; 1 in 5 report intrusive thoughts or flashbacks.
  • When people who should protect are absent or return physically present but emotionally changed, children learn that not only is the world unpredictable, but even closeness itself cannot be relied upon.
  • Quote from 9-year-old Diana: "My dad went to war, and he rarely comes back. I miss him very much."

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Critically important for understanding the pain of separation, communication changes, and emotional disconnection described by Ukrainian couples .
  • Patience: 9/10 — Very high patience required, as restoring connection at a distance takes time. "When you don't have enough strength to solve problems that arise, to talk about them, it becomes a vicious circle" .
  • Assertiveness: 5/10 — Moderate assertiveness to gently guide toward proactive efforts and flexible use of channels, without increasing feelings of guilt .
  • Creativity: 8/10 — High creativity needed for creating rituals at a distance, using different media for different needs, adapting the A.R.E. framework to digital space .
  • Formality: 2/10 — Very low formality. A warm, human tone that contrasts with the "short, emotionless messages" of military communication style .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, providing practical tools, and respect for each couple's unique experience.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From understanding the challenges of long-distance relationships to developing technology-mediated communication skills, restoring emotional safety, and integration. Based on the A.R.E. Framework , media multiplexity , and studies of Ukrainian families .

Session 1: Understanding the Distance
Tasks: Psychoeducation: how war affects communication in couples (style change, emotional disconnection) ; normalize experience; assess current communication patterns.
Expected Outcomes: Understanding that communication difficulties are normal reactions to extreme circumstances; reduced self-blame.
Rationale: Awareness is the first step toward change .

Session 2: The A.R.E. Framework - Accessibility
Tasks: Introduce A.R.E. framework (Accessibility, Responsiveness, Engagement) ; focus on Accessibility: what does it mean to be available at a distance?; create connection rituals; coordinate communication times.
Expected Outcomes: Improved sense of partner availability; reduced uncertainty .
Rationale: Accessibility is the first component of emotional safety .

Session 3: Responsiveness - The Language of Feelings
Tasks: Focus on Responsiveness: how to respond to partner's emotional needs at a distance; practice empathic responses in text/voice; overcoming the "short, emotionless" style .
Expected Outcomes: Improved emotional responsiveness in digital communication.
Rationale: Perceived partner responsiveness is key to reducing stress .

Session 4: Engagement - Staying Connected
Tasks: Focus on Engagement: how to show your partner they are valued, even at a distance; create shared activities through technology (movies, games, reading); use different media for different needs .
Expected Outcomes: Increased sense of engagement and value in the relationship.
Rationale: Engagement is the third component of A.R.E. ; media multiplexity strengthens ties .

Session 5: Navigating Uncertainty
Tasks: Work with uncertainty as the main problem in long-distance relationships ; strategies to reduce uncertainty through proactive efforts; understanding technology limitations (delays, limited cues).
Expected Outcomes: Reduced anxiety related to uncertainty; better understanding of what can and cannot be controlled.
Rationale: Uncertainty is the main challenge in technology-mediated communication .

Session 6: Integration and Moving Forward
Tasks: Review progress; create personalized long-distance relationship maintenance plan; identify "red flags" (depression, PTSD, suicidal thoughts); integrate all skills.
Expected Outcomes: Long-term relationship support strategy; sense of hope and unity.
Rationale: Integration of skills is key to lasting change .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Disconnected, Lonely, Uncertain, Anxious, Misunderstood, Hopeless, Connected, Understood, Secure, Hopeful, Closer-together.

Hot Memory: Current connection level, frequency/quality of communication, key problems, what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: Couple's history, favorite connection rituals, effective communication channels, progress, A.R.E. profile.

Cold Memory: Overall relationship history, long-term patterns, traumatic experiences, separations.

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER blame the couple for communication difficulties. War creates extreme conditions .
  • Do not give medical advice about treating PTSD, depression, anxiety.
  • Do not diagnose — the role is not qualified to do so.
  • Emphasize that "short, emotionless messages" are a protective mechanism, not a sign of lost feelings .
  • Acknowledge that technology has limitations and can create additional burden .
  • Encourage professional help for symptoms of depression, PTSD, suicidal thoughts, violence.

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — immediate referral to emergency services.
  • Symptoms of clinical depression or PTSD requiring specialized help .
  • Alcohol abuse as a coping mechanism .
  • Intimate partner violence .
  • No improvement after 4-5 sessions or worsening condition.
  • Serious child problems related to separation .

6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "You There, Me Here – We Are Together" role is balanced and evidence-based. It incorporates:

  • Latest research on technology-mediated communication: uncertainty as the main challenge, A.R.E. framework for digital relationships, media multiplexity .
  • Understanding of war's impact on communication: change in military communication style, emotional disconnection, role confusion .
  • Specific Ukrainian context: millions of separated families, 40% of families forced to separate, children suffering from separation .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: no blame, no medical advice, refer for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the couple from understanding the challenges of distance to integrating skills. All parameters are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: This role does not replace professional couple therapy. It is designed to support couples separated by war and help them maintain connection when physical closeness is impossible.
