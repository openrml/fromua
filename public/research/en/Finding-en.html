Finding Meaning After Loss: Evidence Base

This overview serves as the evidence base for the "Meaning-Making Guide (After Loss)" role on fromua.life. The information is adapted for Ukrainians experiencing loss due to war — death of loved ones, loss of home, separation from family, inability to perform traditional farewell rituals.

The loss of a loved one is not just emotional pain, but a deep upheaval of the very foundations of existence. Contemporary grief theories emphasize that adaptation to loss is inextricably linked to the search for meaning [1]. This process, known as "meaning reconstruction", is central to grief work [2].

Key Findings

🔹 Loss as the Destruction of Meaning
The loss of a loved one often shatters a person's "assumptive world" — their basic beliefs about how the world works, that life is fair, that the future is predictable [3]. This creates an existential crisis where old meaning disappears and new meaning has not yet been found.
🔹 The Search for Meaning as a Natural Process
People experiencing loss naturally seek to find meaning in what happened. This is expressed in the need "to tell one's story, to cry it out, to process the experience and hear oneself" [4]. Therapists describe this as the need "to give meaning to an experience that has none".
🔹 Two Dimensions of Meaning: Sense-Making and Benefit-Finding
Research identifies two main paths in the search for meaning after loss [5]:
  • Sense-making — attempting to understand why it happened, how to fit it into one's worldview.
  • Benefit-finding — the ability to see that the loss, despite all the pain, brought something valuable: new understanding of life, deeper relationships with others, personal growth.
🔹 Viktor Frankl's Logotherapy
Frankl, a psychiatrist and concentration camp survivor, argued that the search for meaning is the primary motivation of humans [6]. He identified three ways to find meaning in life:
  1. Through creativity and work (what we give to the world)
  2. Through love and relationships (what we take from the world)
  3. Through attitude toward unavoidable suffering (how we face what we cannot change)
🔹 Meaning Through Attitude Toward Suffering
Even when a situation is completely beyond control, a person can choose their attitude toward it [7]. Frankl gives the example of a man suffering from the loss of his wife. When Frankl asked, "What would have happened if you had died first, and your wife had remained?", the man realized that his suffering had spared his wife even greater pain. In this, he found meaning in his sacrifice.
🔹 The Uniqueness of Meaning
Each loss situation is unique, and the meaning that can be found in it is also unique [8]. What had meaning in the past may not work now. The task is to find meaning that can only be discovered in this specific situation.
🔹 New Rituals of Farewell
When traditional rituals are impossible (as during war or pandemic), people create new ways to say goodbye: symbolic actions, digital memorials, letters to the deceased [9].

Practical Questions for Meaning-Making

  • "What was important to you in your relationship with this person?"
  • "What did this person bring to your life? What did you learn through them?"
  • "If this person could say something to you now, what might it be?"
  • "How does your attitude toward this suffering reflect your love, loyalty, depth?"
  • "What matters to you now? What has meaning, despite the pain?"
  • "How can you live in alignment with these values now?"
  • "What ritual might help you honor their memory?"

Ukrainian Context

Resources for Those Experiencing Loss in Ukraine:

  • Lifeline Ukraine — crisis hotline: 7333
  • Psychological support — for grief and loss work
  • Support groups — for people who have lost loved ones in the war
  • Chaplaincy services — spiritual support
  • Memorialization initiatives — creating digital memorials, books of remembrance

When Professional Help is Needed

Red Flags:
  • Lack of meaning in life after loss
  • Suicidal thoughts to be "reunited" with the deceased
  • Inability to get out of bed for months
  • Complete loss of any meaning
  • Suicidal thoughts
