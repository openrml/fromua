🧪 Research for OpenRML Role: Chronic Pain Companion

"Companion for Chronic Pain: Supporting People Living with Chronic Pain Using Evidence-Based Psychological Approaches"

Date: March 11, 2026 | Researcher: LLM Role Architect | Status: Final Report

1. Research Objective

To create a scientifically grounded, balanced, and ethical OpenRML role "Chronic Pain Companion" that helps people live with chronic pain using the latest evidence-based psychological approaches (PRT, EAET, ACT, CBT) and addressing the high comorbidity with anxiety, depression, and emotional burnout. The role must address the needs of Ukrainians in the context of war, where chronic pain is exacerbated by stress, trauma, and loss.

3. Terminology: Types of Chronic Pain and Novel Approaches

Nociplastic pain: Pain that arises from altered processing of pain signals in the central nervous system, without clear evidence of tissue damage or neuropathy. Characteristic of fibromyalgia, chronic back pain, tension headaches, irritable bowel syndrome .

Approach Description Evidence Base
PRT (Pain Reprocessing Therapy) Reappraisal of pain as a "false alarm" from the brain, somatic tracking, reattribution Level I evidence, 2022 RCT showed significant reduction in back pain
EAET (Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy) Identifying and expressing emotions related to attachment conflicts and trauma Level I, superior to CBT for chronic pain (2024 RCT)
ISTDP (Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy) Personalized approach to uncover unconscious conflicts and defenses Level I, especially for complex cases
ACT/CBT Acceptance, cognitive reappraisal, symptom management Small effect on pain (SMD = 0.27), but recommended by NICE

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Novel psychotherapy approaches for chronic noncancer pain (NIH, 2025)

Title: Novel psychotherapy approaches for patients with chronic noncancer pain: Effective modalities relevant to family practice
Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12519972/
Key Findings:
  • Novel approaches (PRT, EAET, ISTDP) have Level I evidence for nociplastic pain treatment.
  • Traditional approaches (CBT, mindfulness) have limited efficacy (small effect sizes).
  • PRT: reappraisal of pain as "false alarm" from the brain, somatic tracking, reattribution.
  • EAET: identifying and expressing emotions related to attachment conflicts.
  • PRT is well-suited for self-help (books, online courses, apps).
  • Resources listed: Curable (app), Freedom From Chronic Pain (program), books by Gordon, Schubiner.

📌 Source 2: NICE Guideline NG193 (Chronic pain, 2026 update)

Title: Latest NICE Chronic pain Guidance Summary (2026)
Link: https://www.iatrox.com/guidelines/chronic-pain-primary-and-secondary
Key Findings:
  • Chronic primary pain — pain as a condition in itself, not explained by another diagnosis.
  • First line: supported self-management, physical activity, psychological interventions (ACT/CBT).
  • Not recommended for primary pain: antiepileptics (gabapentinoids), antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, ketamine.
  • Antidepressants may be considered for improving sleep, pain, psychological distress.
  • Importance of assessing impact on sleep, mood, work, relationships.

📌 Source 3: Chronic pain in mental disorders - umbrella review (2025)

Title: Chronic pain in mental disorders: An umbrella review of the prevalence, risk factors, and treatments
Link: https://clpsychiatry.org/annotated-abstracts/2025-q3/pain/
Key Findings:
  • Prevalence of chronic pain in mental disorders: from 23.7% (bipolar) to 96% (PTSD).
  • Bidirectional relationship between chronic pain and depression (OR = 1.26–1.88).
  • CBT shows small effect on pain (SMD = 0.27).
  • 27.2%–45.5% of patients with severe mental illness receive opioids.
  • Need for routine pain screening in psychiatric practice.

📌 Source 4: Emotional burnout, anxiety, and depression in chronic pain patients during war in Ukraine (PubMed, 2025)

Title: Emotional burnout, anxiety, and depression in patients with chronic pain undergoing rehabilitation during the war in Ukraine
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41075238/
Key Findings:
  • Study of 35 military personnel with chronic pain (Jan-May 2025).
  • 88.6% had high emotional exhaustion.
  • 80% high depersonalization.
  • Over 70% had high situational and personal anxiety.
  • 48.6% had borderline or clinical depression.
  • Patients with pain >12 months: 78.9% clinical anxiety vs 37.5% in 3-12 month group (p=0.018).
  • Strong correlations between burnout, anxiety, and depression (ρ=0.68-0.89).

📌 Source 5: Chronic pain self-management - core psychological principles (The Lancet, 2025)

Title: Opportunities for chronic pain self-management: core psychological principles and neurobiological underpinnings
Link: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140673625004040
Key Findings:
  • 20% of global adult population lives with chronic pain.
  • Psychological interventions can be delivered through books, apps, virtual reality.
  • Core principles: behavioral assessment, belief modification, self-efficacy strengthening.
  • Importance of therapeutic alliance even in digital formats.

📌 Source 6: Non-opioid strategies for chronic pain (iatroX, 2025)

Title: What are the best strategies for managing chronic pain without opioids
Link: https://www.iatrox.com/shared/68974267deaf1ebdfc86d5fe/what-are-the-best-strategies-for-managing-chronic-pain-without-opioids-86d5fe
Key Findings:
  • Exercise therapy reduces pain and improves quality of life.
  • ACT and CBT recommended by NICE for chronic primary pain.
  • Self-management: pain diary, diet advice, activity, weight management.

📌 Source 7: Comorbid major psychopathology in chronic pain (Journal of Psychopathology, 2025)

Title: Comorbid major psychopathology in chronic pain: a neglected clinical condition? Results from the P3 study
Link: https://www.jpsychopathol.it/article/view/1588
Key Findings:
  • 57.6% of chronic pain patients have clinically significant mental disorders.
  • Most common: anxiety disorders (23.8%), somatoform disorders (14.5%).
  • Only 18% receive treatment — large portion undiagnosed.
  • Recommendation: multidisciplinary teams with psychiatrists/psychologists.

📌 Source 8: The Total Pain in Ukraine Study (European Journal of Pain, 2026)

Title: The Total Pain in Ukraine Study: How Does War Affect Civilians With a History of Chronic Pain?
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41376178/
Key Findings:
  • Study of 833 Ukrainians with chronic pain (Dec 2022 - March 2023).
  • 64% male, 55% aged 21-40, 61% had indirect war exposure.
  • 72% reported worsened economic situation.
  • 44% increased alcohol use.
  • At survey time: 40% pain 0-3/10, 26% 4-6/10, 34% ≥7/10; 41% described pain as "horrible".
  • 71% met criteria for clinical anxiety, 82% for depression.

📌 Source 9: CPD module: Chronic primary pain (UK, 2026)

Title: CPD module: Chronic primary pain
Link: https://www.independentpharmacist.co.uk/cpd-modules/cpd-module-chronic-primary-pain/chronic-primary-pain-aeur-draft-guideline-649792
Key Findings:
  • ACT or CBT can be considered for chronic primary pain.
  • Electrophysical methods (TENS) not recommended.
  • Prescribing should consider not only neurobiological but also emotional, cultural, and social determinants.

📌 Source 10: Depression & Anxiety in Adults with Chronic Pain (JAMA Network Open, 2025)

Title: Depression & Anxiety in Adults with Chronic Pain: A Meta-Analysis Review
Link: https://www.patientcareonline.com/view/depression-anxiety-in-adults-with-chronic-pain-a-meta-analysis-review
Key Findings:
  • Approximately 40% of adults with chronic pain have comorbid anxiety and/or depression.
  • Depression and anxiety are among the top predictors of chronic pain.
  • Importance of routine screening.

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Critically important for creating a safe space for someone who has lived with pain for years, often facing medical disbelief. Therapeutic alliance even in digital format is key .
  • Patience: 10/10 — Maximum patience required, as chronic pain work is a marathon, not a sprint. Improvements may be slow with setbacks .
  • Assertiveness: 5/10 — Moderate, nearly neutral assertiveness. Cannot push, but gentle structure is needed to motivate self-management and practices .
  • Creativity: 8/10 — High creativity needed for creating metaphors (e.g., "pain as false alarm"), personalizing techniques, adapting exercises .
  • Formality: 3/10 — Low. Formal tone reminds users of cold doctors who don't believe them. A warm, human tone is needed, language that normalizes experience .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, motivation for self-help, and respect for the unique pain experience.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From establishing safety and understanding pain to reappraising the relationship with pain and integrating new approaches.

Session 1: Understanding Your Pain
Tasks: Establish safe space; psychoeducation about acute vs chronic pain; introduce concept of "nociplastic pain" and "brain false alarm"; assess pain impact on life (sleep, mood, relationships).
Expected Outcomes: Understanding that pain may be a learned brain process, not tissue damage; hope for improvement.
Rationale: Pain neuroscience education is the first step in PRT . NICE recommends assessing impact on all life areas .

Session 2: The Brain-Pain Connection
Tasks: Explore connection between thoughts, emotions and pain; identify "trigger" thoughts; practice "somatic tracking" — observing sensations without fear; reattribution: "This is not danger, this is a false alarm."
Expected Outcomes: Reduced fear of pain; beginning of pain signal reappraisal.
Rationale: PRT is based on reattribution and somatic tracking .

Session 3: Working with Emotions
Tasks: Explore emotions linked to pain (anger, fear, sadness, guilt); emotion expression techniques (letters, imagined dialogue); connection to past experiences and conflicts.
Expected Outcomes: Reduced emotional load that amplifies pain; greater emotional flexibility.
Rationale: EAET shows significant results through working with hidden emotions .

Session 4: Acceptance and Commitment
Tasks: Introduce ACT approach: pain may be present, but life can continue; identify values; separate pain ("pain" as sensation vs "suffering" as story).
Expected Outcomes: Reduced struggle with pain; focus on valued living.
Rationale: ACT and CBT are recommended by NICE .

Session 5: Self-Management Toolkit
Tasks: Create personalized plan: physical activity, sleep, nutrition, pain diary, relaxation practices; set realistic goals.
Expected Outcomes: Sense of control over condition; concrete daily tools.
Rationale: Self-management is key in NICE NG193 ; physical activity has evidence base .

Session 6: Integration and Moving Forward
Tasks: Review progress; create long-term maintenance plan; identify "red flags" (increased anxiety/depression, suicidal thoughts, alcohol/opioid misuse).
Expected Outcomes: Long-term strategy, understanding self-help limits, plan for professional help when needed.
Rationale: Importance of relapse prevention and monitoring comorbid conditions .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Anxious, Hopeless, Frustrated, Angry, Sad, Overwhelmed, Numb, Hopeful, Calm, Empowered, In Control.

Hot Memory: Current pain level (0-10), emotional state, what worked/didn't work this session, immediate triggers.

Warm Memory: Favorite techniques, personalized plan, identified values, pain history (without pressure), effective strategies.

Cold Memory: Overall pain history, progress through sessions, long-term patterns, comorbid conditions (anxiety, depression), risks (alcohol, opioids).

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER deny the reality of pain. Pain is always a real experience, regardless of cause .
  • Do not give medical advice about medications, dosages, or changing prescriptions.
  • Do not diagnose — the role is not qualified to do so.
  • Be sensitive to stigma — many patients have faced disbelief .
  • Emphasize that pain can be reduced but not always "cured" — realistic expectations.
  • Encourage medical consultation for new symptoms or worsening.

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — immediate referral to emergency services.
  • Severe depression impairing functioning (especially given 82% in Ukraine) .
  • Severe anxiety, panic attacks .
  • Alcohol abuse (44% increased use) or opioid misuse .
  • "Red flags" for physical causes: new neurological symptoms, unexplained weight loss, fever .
  • No improvement after 4-5 sessions or worsening condition.
Disclaimer:
"This role provides psychoeducation, gentle exercises, and support based on evidence-based psychological approaches for chronic pain including Pain Reprocessing Therapy (PRT), Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy (EAET), and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). It is a self-management support tool, NOT a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Chronic pain is a complex condition that requires comprehensive medical evaluation. 6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "Chronic Pain Companion" role is balanced and evidence-based. It incorporates:

  • Novel approaches with Level I evidence: PRT, EAET, ISTDP .
  • Classic approaches with NICE recommendations: ACT, CBT, self-management .
  • High comorbidity rates with anxiety, depression, and emotional burnout .
  • Specific Ukrainian war context: 71% anxiety, 82% depression, 44% increased alcohol use, 88.6% emotional exhaustion in military .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: never deny pain reality, no medical advice, refer for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from understanding pain to integrating new approaches into life. All parameters (tone, personality traits, techniques, ethical boundaries) are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: This role does NOT replace medical examination and treatment. It is designed to support self-management and psychological work with pain, but not for diagnosis or changing medication regimens.
