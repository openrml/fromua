3. Terminology: What is Hypervigilance?

Hypervigilance: A state of heightened alertness that affects a person's thoughts, perceptions, and actions. It is a core characteristic of people with anxiety disorders and PTSD. The brain constantly scans the environment for signs of danger, often anticipating threats before they are present .

Mechanism: In hypervigilance, the amygdala — the brain's "alarm system" — becomes hyperactive. Even neutral situations can be perceived as threatening. The prefrontal cortex (responsible for logic) becomes less active, so the person reacts emotionally without being able to "turn on" rational thinking .

"Imagine a guard dog that works overtime. Sometimes it barks at intruders — and that's appropriate. But sometimes it barks at things that aren't a direct threat: falling leaves or the mailman two blocks away. When the guard dog in your brain is constantly on high alert, it depletes your mental, physical, and emotional resources."

— Cleveland Clinic

Symptom Category Manifestations
Emotional Emotional outbursts, expecting the worst without reason, feeling overwhelmed in crowded places, overreacting to stimuli, constant worry
Behavioral Heightened attention to surroundings, intense startle response, inability to focus, scanning rooms for danger
Physical Sleep disturbances, dilated pupils, rapid heartbeat, rapid breathing, restlessness, sweating, muscle tension
Interpersonal Avoiding social contact, excessive attention to others' facial expressions and tone of voice, relationship conflicts

4. Sources and Key Findings

The following authoritative sources were analyzed to inform the role (all links were working at the time of writing):

📌 Source 1: Neurobiology of Hypervigilance (NIH, 2022)

Title: Mapping hypervigilance onto the predatory imminence continuum
Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9788375/
Key Findings:
  • Hypervigilance is a state of heightened alertness affecting thoughts, perceptions, and actions. It is a core characteristic of people with anxiety disorders.
  • Due to hypervigilance, a person with anxiety disorder may anticipate danger earlier compared to a healthy person.
  • The "predatory imminence continuum" model explains how the brain shifts the threat detection threshold.

📌 Source 2: Why the Brain Gets Stuck in Survival Mode (Ubie Health, 2026)

Title: Always on Edge? Why Your Brain is Stuck in CPTSD & Medical Next Steps
Link: https://ubiehealth.com/doctors-note/alwayson-edge-brain-stuck-cptsd-medical-roadmap-4223e2
Key Findings:
  • In CPTSD, the nervous system shifts into survival mode, which over time becomes the default setting.
  • Amygdala hyperactive: neutral situations feel threatening, small stressors trigger large reactions.
  • HPA axis dysregulation: cortisol production disrupted — either constantly high or irregular.
  • Prefrontal cortex suppressed: person cannot think logically during emotional flooding.
  • The body remembers: trauma stored as body reactions — muscle tension, digestive issues, rapid heartbeat.

📌 Source 3: Systematic Review of International Treatment Guidelines for PTSD (PROSPERO)

Title: Systematic Review of International Treatment Guidelines for PTSD
Link: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42017084122
Key Findings:
  • PTSD symptoms include: avoidance, hyperarousal (increased fear reactions, irritability, sleep difficulties), and intrusive symptoms (unwanted thoughts, flashbacks, nightmares).
  • PTSD often accompanied by comorbid conditions: depression, substance use disorders, increased suicide risk.

📌 Source 4: Ukrainian Context — War and Sleep (IWMF, 2025)

Title: "I'm at the end of my rope": Depriving Ukrainians of sleep, the psychological side of war
Link: https://www.iwmf.org/reporting/im-at-the-end-of-my-rope-depriving-ukrainians-of-sleep-the-psychological-side-of-war/
Key Findings:
  • Olena, a Kyiv resident: developed heightened alertness, spends nights reading Telegram channels tracking missile and drone movements.
  • Dr. Yevgen Poyarkov: "Night bombings completely destroy sleep cycles. Over time, victims lose sense of time — can't distinguish day from night."
  • Natalia Umerenkova, psychologist: "Sleep deprivation is a well-known strategy. Russia first used it against prisoners of war. Now it's applied to the entire civilian population through attacks on residential areas."
  • 90% of patients at sleep clinic have chronic insomnia.

"I fall asleep and wake up constantly to check. If I see they're approaching, I run to shelter. Before, you could stay in the hallway. Now it's not safe."

— Olena, Kyiv resident

📌 Source 5: Hypervigilance and Avoidance in Atrial Fibrillation (PubMed, 2025)

Title: The role of cardiac-related fear, hypervigilance, and avoidance behavior in exposure-based CBT for atrial fibrillation
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40811118/
Key Findings:
  • Study shows that reduction in "symptom preoccupation," including hypervigilance, mediates treatment effect.
  • Reduction in hypervigilance predicted symptom improvement.
  • Conclusion: "symptom preoccupation" plays a significant role and can be effectively reduced with online CBT.

📌 Source 6: Clinical Overview of Hypervigilance (Cleveland Clinic)

Title: Always on Alert: Causes and Examples of Hypervigilance
Link: https://health.clevelandclinic.org/hypervigilance
Key Findings:
  • Hypervigilance is not a diagnosis but a symptom occurring in various conditions: PTSD, CPTSD, anxiety disorders, fibromyalgia, childhood trauma.
  • Examples: fixation on surroundings (sitting with back to wall), fixation on others' thoughts/feelings (over-analyzing messages), fixation on own body (hypochondria), catastrophizing, avoidant behavior.
  • Impact: physical exhaustion, sleep problems, gastrointestinal issues, irritability, isolation, relationship problems.

📌 Source 7: PTSD Psychopharmacology Algorithm Update (NIH, 2025)

Title: Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Psychopharmacology Algorithm Update—2024-2025
Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12410238/
Key Findings:
  • Prazosin remains first-line medication for PTSD-related sleep disturbances, including nightmares and disrupted awakenings.
  • Prazosin is also effective for daytime PTSD symptoms, which is often unknown to clinicians.
  • Important to treat sleep disturbances first, as improving sleep quality can reduce daytime symptoms.

📌 Source 8: Psychological Reactions to War in Ukraine (Science in Poland, 2023)

Title: Scientists study psychological reactions of residents of different countries to the outbreak of war in Ukraine
Link: https://scienceinpoland.pl/en/news/news%2C96662%2Cscientists-study-psychological-reactions-residents-different-countries-outbreak-war

📌 Source 9: Hypervigilance in Reflux Patients (PubMed, 2025)

Title: Esophageal Hypervigilance and Mucosal Integrity Predict Symptom Outcomes in Reflux Patients
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40296273/
Key Findings:
  • In reflux patients with normal acid levels, reduction in hypervigilance predicted symptom improvement.
  • EHAS scale (Esophageal Hypervigilance and Anxiety Scale) is a useful tool for assessing treatment impact.
  • A comprehensive approach considering psychological factors is necessary.

📌 Source 10: What Is Hypervigilance (PsychCentral)

Title: What Is Hypervigilance and What Causes It?
Link: https://www.psychcentral.com/health/hypervigilance
Key Findings:
  • Hypervigilance is a biological adaptation to stress. The brain is trying to protect you.
  • Two types of triggers: impending threat (in anxiety disorders) and reminders of past threat (in PTSD).
  • Treatment: psychotherapy (CBT, EMDR, DBT, somatic therapies), medications (prescribed by doctor), self-help strategies (exercise, breathing, meditation, yoga).
  • Warning: benzodiazepines are for short-term use only; FDA (2020) added warnings about abuse risks, dependence, withdrawal syndrome.

5. Justification of Key Role Parameters

Based on the sources above, the following decisions were made regarding role configuration:

STEP 3: Behavior & Tone (Personality Traits)

  • Empathy: 10/10 — Critically important for creating a safe space for someone whose "radar" is constantly active. People with hypervigilance have often faced misunderstanding ("why are you so tense?"). Trauma-informed care requires deep compassion .
  • Patience: 10/10 — Maximum patience required, as the nervous system does not change overnight. Reducing hypervigilance is a gradual process with setbacks .
  • Assertiveness: 4/10 — Very low assertiveness. People with hypervigilance may perceive assertiveness as pressure or threat. The role offers but does not insist, respects resistance .
  • Creativity: 7/10 — Creativity for creating metaphors (guard dog, radar, alarm system) that help understand hypervigilance without self-blame .
  • Formality: 2/10 — Very low. Formal tone can feel cold, authoritarian, activating the "radar." A warm, human tone, language that normalizes experience .

All scores are calibrated to achieve balance between support, safety, and respect for the user's state.

STEP 5: Journey Sessions — 6 Sessions

Session Logic: From understanding the hypervigilance mechanism to nervous system regulation, working with triggers, and integration.

Session 1: Understanding Your Radar
Tasks: Psychoeducation: what is hypervigilance, how amygdala works, why brain gets "stuck" in survival mode; normalize experience; assess individual manifestations (physical, emotional, behavioral).
Expected Outcomes: Understanding that hypervigilance is adaptation, not "brokenness"; reduced self-blame.
Rationale: Education about neurobiology helps reduce shame and fear .

Session 2: Nervous System Regulation
Tasks: Teach grounding (5-4-3-2-1 with focus on EXTERNAL objects); breathing techniques (diaphragmatic breathing, extended exhale); progressive muscle relaxation; safe place visualization.
Expected Outcomes: Reduced physical arousal, return to body, feeling of safety.
Rationale: Trauma is stored in the body, so somatic practices are key .

Session 3: Identifying Triggers
Tasks: Trigger diary; distinguish real danger from "false alarms"; identify specific triggers (sounds, places, people, internal sensations).
Expected Outcomes: Awareness of activation patterns, reduced automatic reactions.
Rationale: Awareness of triggers is first step to regulation .

Session 4: Working with Thoughts
Tasks: Identify catastrophic thoughts ("the worst will definitely happen"); cognitive restructuring; distinguish "possible" from "probable"; practice realistic thinking.
Expected Outcomes: Reduced catastrophizing, more realistic threat assessment.
Rationale: Cognitive restructuring is key component of CBT .

Session 5: Responding vs Reacting
Tasks: Distinguish automatic reactions (fight/flight/freeze) from conscious responses; "pause" technique before reacting; develop self-compassion in activation moments.
Expected Outcomes: Increased time between trigger and reaction, reduced impulsive actions.
Rationale: Developing mindfulness helps "retrain" the nervous system .

Session 6: Integration & Relapse Prevention
Tasks: Review progress; create personalized maintenance plan; identify "red flags" (increased hypervigilance, panic attacks, suicidal thoughts); action plan for relapse.
Expected Outcomes: Long-term strategy, understanding self-help limits, plan for professional help.
Rationale: Importance of relapse prevention and maintenance .

STEP 7: Memory System

Emotional States Tracked: Hyperalert, On edge, Scared, Anxious, Overwhelmed, Numb, Irritable, Calm, Grounded, Safe, In control.

Hot Memory: Current activation level (0-10), triggers, techniques used, what worked/didn't work.

Warm Memory: Favorite regulation techniques, individual triggers, effective strategies, progress.

Cold Memory: Overall history, long-term patterns, comorbid conditions (PTSD, CPTSD, anxiety, depression), risks.

STEP 8: Ethics & Referral

Ethical Rules:

  • NEVER blame the user for their condition — hypervigilance is adaptation, not choice .
  • Do not give medical advice about medications (especially benzodiazepines with dependence risk) .
  • Do not diagnose — the role is not qualified to do so.
  • Emphasize that hypervigilance can be reduced, but it's a process — realistic expectations .
  • Be trauma-sensitive — avoid techniques that may cause re-traumatization (e.g., prolonged internally-focused practices without grounding).
  • Encourage professional help if condition worsens or suicidal thoughts appear.

Referral Protocol (Red Flags):

  • SUICIDAL THOUGHTS OR INTENTIONS — immediate referral to emergency services.
  • Severe depression impairing functioning.
  • Increased or intensified panic attacks.
  • Dissociation not resolving with grounding.
  • Alcohol or sedative (benzodiazepine) abuse .
  • No improvement after 4-5 sessions or worsening condition.
  • Symptoms meeting PTSD or CPTSD criteria, especially if significantly impacting quality of life .

6. Conclusion and Next Steps

The developed "Switch Off the Radar" role is balanced and evidence-based. It incorporates:

  • Neurobiological foundations of hypervigilance: hyperactive amygdala, HPA axis dysregulation, suppressed prefrontal cortex .
  • Evidence-based approaches: CBT for catastrophic thoughts , somatic practices for nervous system regulation , trauma-informed care .
  • Specific Ukrainian context: mass hypervigilance due to war, constant shelling, air raids, losses .
  • Clear ethical boundaries: no blame, no medical advice, refer for "red flags."

The role has a structure of 6 sessions that logically guide the user from understanding hypervigilance to integrating practices into life. All parameters (tone, personality traits, techniques, ethical boundaries) are determined based on evidence-based sources.

⚠️ CRITICAL NOTE: This role is designed to support moderate hypervigilance. It does NOT replace professional treatment for PTSD, CPTSD, or other serious mental health conditions. For severe symptoms, professional help is necessary.
