Parenting During War: Evidence Base

This overview serves as the evidence base for the "Parenting During War Guide" role on fromua.life. The information is adapted for Ukrainian families experiencing war — evacuation, loss of home, separation from loved ones, constant stress.

War creates unique challenges for parents: they must support their children while coping with their own stress and trauma. Research shows that even in these conditions, parents can be a powerful resource for their children [1].

Key Findings

🔹 Self-Care is the First Step
Children, especially younger ones, look to their parents' reactions. When parents are visibly frightened or upset, children feel less protected. It's important to have a "safe place" for your own emotions (friends, psychologist) and daily recovery practices (prayer, meditation, breathing) [2].
🔹 Parental Emotional Support Protects Children, Even When Parents Are Traumatized
A 2025 study found: the more parents encourage open dialogue about emotions, legitimize negative feelings, and help find solutions, the more resilient children are [3]. This connection holds even when parents themselves have PTSD symptoms. You don't need to be a "perfect parent" — what matters is listening and giving space for feelings.
🔹 Understanding Age-Specific Reactions
Children's emotions often manifest through behavior: tantrums, regressive behavior (bedwetting, thumb-sucking), aggression, or social isolation. It's important to know typical age-specific reactions to trauma in order to respond appropriately [4].

Age-Specific Reactions to Trauma:

Children under 5: crying, screaming, trembling, helplessness, excessive dependence on parents, regressive behavior (return to pacifier, wet pants), sleep and appetite disturbances, fear of separation.
Children 6-11 years: disorientation, confusion, irritability, anger outbursts, frequent arguments with peers, headaches and stomachaches, social isolation, school problems, behavioral regression.
Adolescents 12-18 years: shock, fear, guilt, shame, anxiety, apathy, risky behavior (alcohol, drugs), sleep and eating disorders, loss of interest in usual activities, suicidal thoughts.

Practical Recommendations

✅ Talking with Your Child
Talk to children about the situation in an age-appropriate way. For younger children, reminding them they are safe is enough. With older children, discuss openly, ask what they know and feel, listen without judgment. It's okay to admit you don't know the answer to a question [5].
✅ Routine as a Lifeline
Daily routine creates a sense of predictability and safety. Regular sleep, meals, hygiene, shared rituals help maintain a sense of order and control [6].
✅ Time Together and Physical Closeness
Extra hugs, reading books, playing together, cooking meals remind the child that parents are nearby and love them [7].
✅ Limiting Media
Watching distressing news and images of violence increases fear and anxiety. Limit exposure, especially for younger children, and watch news with older children to be able to answer questions [8].

Three Phases of Support

A 2025 study identifies three critical phases for family support [9]:

  1. Escalation of Hostilities — need for safety, evacuation, shelter
  2. Evacuation and Displacement — loss of home, separation, adapting to a new place
  3. Returning Home — recovery, facing losses, reintegration

Each phase requires special attention and different support strategies.

When Professional Help is Needed

Red Flags in Children:
  • Constant nightmares, sleep disturbances
  • Regressive behavior that doesn't improve over time
  • Aggression, self-harm
  • Complete social isolation
  • Refusing food
  • Suicidal thoughts (in adolescents)
